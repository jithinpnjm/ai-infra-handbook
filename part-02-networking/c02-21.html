Why this matters for AI infrastructure​

What would an attacker want from a GPU cluster? The answer is more than one thing, and that is why these clusters draw attention. Compute is valuable on its own, because it can be used for cryptomining or resold. Data is valuable too, including training sets, model weights and customer prompts. Privileged access paths such as BMCs, schedulers, registries and CI let an attacker reach everything else. A BMC is the small management computer inside each server, and anyone who controls it controls the machine underneath, even when the operating system is switched off.

The networks are unusual as well. Flat, high-speed RDMA fabrics bypass the host kernel, so the usual host-level checks never see that traffic. Management planes use out-of-band controllers that run their own firmware. Multi-tenant environments let tenants share switches. Fast-moving software stacks, such as drivers, container runtimes and serving frameworks, bring a steady stream of CVEs, which are the public numbers given to known security flaws.

Network security here is not only firewalls. It is also segmentation, identity, key management, least privilege and monitoring. The hard part is applying all of them without strangling performance. A control that halves the speed of an all-reduce will be switched off by someone in a hurry, and then it protects nothing.

The path through this chapter follows the order in which you would think about a real cluster. First comes the vocabulary and the habit of asking who might attack and why. Then come the defensive layers, the tools you actually type, such as SSH and scanners, the protections for routers and switches, and the zero-trust ideas that replace the old trusted-inside model. The last part looks at what changes in AI clusters: BMC and management isolation, RDMA and InfiniBand partitioning, tenant isolation, secrets, and the habit of running security as a loop and not a one-time project. Linux firewall mechanics, nftables and conntrack, are in chapter 2.23. TLS and PKI are in chapter 2.10, and kernel-level hardening is in chapter 1.21.

Decide early which of these assets you are protecting, because that choice changes the controls you need.

Prerequisites and outcomes​

You should be comfortable with a few earlier topics: TCP/IP (2.4 to 2.7), TLS and PKI (2.10), NAT and DHCP (2.9), and Linux user and permission basics. Concretely, you should know how a TCP connection starts, what a certificate proves, how NAT hides an internal address, and why a Linux user without root cannot read another user's files.

Check these prerequisites first, because the SSH and threat-model sections assume them. Every security term that follows is defined when it first appears, always next to an example of how it goes wrong.

1. Vocabulary and threat modelling​

Picture a locked office with a cleaner who holds a key, a visitor with a badge, and a window left slightly open. Each is a different kind of risk, and each needs a different response. Security work becomes manageable when everyone uses the same words for these risks. This section gives those words, and it asks you to draw the building before you decide where the locks go.

Security conversations go wrong fast when people use words loosely. One engineer says "vulnerability" and means a break-in, and another means a missing patch. Before you can protect anything, you need to know who might attack, where the weak points are, and what is worth protecting. The table defines the terms this chapter uses.

TermMeaning
Threat actorWho attacks: individual, criminal group, insider, state actor; identity hints at motive, skill and likely plan
VulnerabilityA weakness: missing filter, default password, unpatched service, misconfiguration, a social-engineerable person
ExploitThe method/tool that uses a vulnerability
Attack / threatThe potential or actual use of an exploit by an actor
Attack surfaceEverything reachable by an attacker: hosts, ports, services, APIs, people, supply chain
AssetWhat matters: data, models, credentials, compute, availability
RiskLikelihood × impact of a negative outcome (breach, downtime, reputational damage)

The terms link together. A vulnerability is the weakness, an exploit is the method that uses it, and an attack is the actual use of that exploit by an actor. Risk combines how likely a bad outcome is with how much it would hurt. A door with a weak lock is a vulnerability, a lock-pick is the exploit, a burglar using it is the attack, and the risk depends on what is behind the door and how likely a burglar is to try.

Three problem areas come up from a network point of view. Area 1 is access: how legitimate users and processes reach the data they need while others cannot. Area 2 is confidentiality and integrity: whether data in transit and at rest stays private and unmodified. Area 3 is availability: whether the network and its services stay usable under denial-of-service attack.

Threat modelling turns those areas into questions you can answer for one system. What are you protecting? Who might attack, and why? How could they get in, and where are the trust boundaries? What would the impact be, and which controls reduce the likelihood or the impact? A simple data-flow diagram helps. You draw where data enters, where it is stored and where it leaves, and mark every line that crosses from a more trusted zone to a less trusted one. The STRIDE categories (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege) work as a checklist at each of those lines. Revisit the model whenever the architecture changes.

A caution on biometrics and "secrets you cannot change". Fingerprints are convenient, but they are not secret, because you leave them on almost every surface you touch. Once a biometric is digitised and stored online, it becomes a permanent credential, and after a breach you cannot rotate it. You can change a leaked password in a minute, but you cannot change your finger. Use biometrics as a convenience factor on a device, where the check stays local and hardware-protected. Do not rely on them as your only remote identity. Treat data exhaust with the same care. That means metadata such as who talks to whom, when, and how much, and it can reveal behaviour even when the payloads are encrypted (chapter 2.10).

Decide which assets matter most before you draw the data-flow diagram, because the threat model depends on that answer.

2. Defense in depth​

Picture a network protected by one firewall and nothing else. If that single control fails, nothing else stands in the way. A medieval castle is a better model: a moat, a wall, a gate, an inner keep, and guards who watch all of them. Defense in depth avoids the single-firewall trap by stacking overlapping controls. No single control stops a determined attacker, but layered controls give you several chances to detect and contain an intrusion, and they make every attack more costly.

Figure 2.21.1. Defense in depth, as a diagram.

Each layer does a different job. At the edge, packet filters and rate limits control what arrives, and route validation guards against hijacked address space. Behind the edge, a stateful packet filter lets in only responses to connections that started inside. Each service adds access control of its own. Telemetry watches all of these layers together, looking for top talkers, anomalies and large, unusual transfers out of sensitive databases.

The time an attacker needs to cross each layer is time for your defenders to notice. More layers also mean more places to apply specific controls. Think of the whole network as one defensive system, in which routers, switches, load balancers, hosts and applications each contribute controls.

Follow an attacker who wants a customer's model weights. They find a forgotten dashboard on the public edge. The edge filter allows it, because the port is open to the world. The stateful firewall lets the first packet in, because it looks like a normal web request. The service behind it asks for a login, and the attacker has a stolen password from an older breach, so the check passes. The service has access to the model store, and the store's network policy allows the connection. Each layer stopped nothing on its own, but each one was a point where a different control could have caught the attacker. Notice that the attack took four passes through the defence, and each pass could have been a detection point.

When you review a design, count the layers an attacker must cross, not just the perimeter.

3. Access control and AAA​

Who gets in, and what can they do once they are in? Access control answers both questions. It checks that users and processes are who they claim to be (authentication). It checks that they may do what they request (authorisation). It records what they did (accounting). Together, these three are called AAA. A bank branch is a good picture: the clerk checks your identity, then checks that your account allows the withdrawal, and then the transaction goes into the ledger.

Access is checked at several points, and you should combine them. Before a device joins the network, port-based access control with 802.1X, Wi-Fi WPA-Enterprise or VPN login can check it. After it connects, network ACLs and segmentation limit where it can go. Before each service, application authentication or mTLS checks the caller. Before the most sensitive resources, privileged access management adds the strongest check. Use stronger factors for more sensitive resources.

The protocols divide this work. RADIUS (UDP 1812 and 1813) handles network access. It uses a shared secret and encrypts only the password field, so protect it with IPsec or RadSec. TACACS+ (TCP 49) handles device administration. It encrypts the whole packet body and supports per-command authorisation and accounting. LDAP, Active Directory and Kerberos back directory-based identity. SAML, OIDC and OAuth2 handle web and API identity, and SPIFFE and SPIRE give workloads their own identity.

Use MFA for administrative access and VPNs, because it combines something you know, something you have and something you are. Hardware keys (FIDO2) resist phishing well. Pair MFA with least privilege and just-in-time access. Least privilege means giving each account only what its job needs, and just-in-time access means granting the rights only for the hours the task takes. Keep admin accounts separate, and avoid shared accounts.

Logging closes the loop. Send central logs through syslog or journald, account for commands on network devices, store logs in immutable storage, and sync time with NTP. Without a shared clock, you cannot line up events from different machines into one story.

Walk through one VPN login from start to finish, because each step is a place where access is decided. The laptop starts the tunnel and presents its certificate, which proves the device is one the company issued. The VPN server asks for a password, then for a one-time code from the phone. Only when both checks pass does the server look up the user's groups. The groups decide which network segments the tunnel may reach. After that, the server logs the login with the time and the source address, so an analyst can later match a session to a person. Each step answers a different question: is the device known, is the person who they claim, what may they reach, and who was it.

Start with administrative access and VPNs when you add MFA, because the chapter names them first for that control.

4. Firewalls and filtering (overview)​

Not every firewall works the same way. A firewall is a checkpoint that decides which packets may pass, and some checkpoints only read the address label while others open the parcel. The table shows the main types, and each one fits a different place in the network.

Figure 2.21.2. A packet is checked against rules from the top; the first match decides.

TypeWhat it doesNotes
Stateless ACL / packet filterMatches header fields per packet (src/dst IP, ports, flags)Fast, in switch hardware; no memory of connections; use at edges and between zones
Stateful firewallTracks connections (conntrack); allows return traffic of permitted flows automatically; drops unsolicited inboundLinux nftables/iptables with ct state; default for host and perimeter firewalls
NGFW / proxy / WAFApplication awareness, IPS signatures, TLS inspection, URL filtering; WAF protects web apps from injectionMore CPU, TLS-inspection privacy/operational trade-offs
Host-based firewallPolicy on each server (nftables, firewalld, ufw), cloud security groupsProtects against lateral movement; enforce by configuration management
Microsegmentation / NetworkPolicyPer-workload policies enforced in the data plane (Kubernetes NetworkPolicy via Calico/Cilium, VXLAN GBP, NSX)Identity-based rather than IP-based rules

Picture a developer whose laptop asks a server on the internet for a package. The reply comes back to the laptop's port. A stateful firewall remembers that the laptop opened the connection, so the reply is allowed in, and nothing else is. Now picture an unknown computer sending a packet to the same laptop without any earlier request. The stateful firewall has no record of it, so it drops the packet. A stateless filter could not make that distinction, because it reads each packet alone. The gap between those two behaviours is why most hosts run a stateful firewall.

Stateless filters are fast, because they run in switch hardware, but they have no memory of connections. Stateful firewalls track each connection and let return traffic back in automatically, which makes them the default for hosts and perimeters. NGFWs, proxies and WAFs look deeper into the application, at the cost of CPU and TLS inspection trade-offs. Host-based firewalls and microsegmentation protect against lateral movement, which is the way an intruder who has taken one machine moves sideways to the next.

Your default stance should be deny. Block all inbound traffic by default, then explicitly allow the services you need. Restrict outbound traffic too, because egress filtering limits exfiltration and command-and-control. Exfiltration is stolen data leaving the building, and command-and-control is the channel an attacker uses to give orders to a compromised machine. Log the drops that matter. Split the network into zones for users, servers, management, storage and the fabric. Place management interfaces such as SSH, BMCs and switch consoles only on a dedicated management network. Review rules regularly, remove unused ones, and test from outside and inside. Chapter 2.23 covers the detailed mechanics and rule examples.

Before you open any port, decide which zone and which management network it belongs to.

5. Data protection: encryption and keys​

Access controls and systems fail, and when they do, encryption is the last line of defense. If a stolen disk or a copied network capture shows only noise, the attacker has gained little. Use encryption in transit, meaning TLS and mTLS, SSH, IPsec, WireGuard and MACsec on links. Use encryption at rest for disks and volumes, object stores and databases. Keys need the same care. Generate them on the device or in an HSM or KMS, rotate them, separate duties, restrict access, never embed them in images or repositories, and audit their use. An HSM is a tamper-resistant hardware box that holds keys and performs cryptographic operations without ever letting the key out, and a KMS is the service that manages such keys.

Model weights and datasets deserve the care you give source code and credentials. Sign your artifacts, control who can reach the registry, keep versions immutable, and record where each artifact came from. That last part is supply-chain security, and it uses a software bill of materials and signature checks on containers and packages.

AI traffic is fast, so encrypting it has a cost. For RDMA fabrics you have three options: MACsec on links (line rate in hardware on some switches and NICs), IPsec offload on NICs and DPUs, or logical isolation combined with physical security. Do not assume TLS inside the fabric is feasible for GPUDirect traffic. Make that threat-model decision on purpose instead. Shared tenants need stronger isolation and encryption, while a single tenant in a locked cage can rely on segmentation and physical controls.

Picture a backup disk that goes missing from a courier van. The disk holds a copy of a training dataset, and the team's first question is whether the data is exposed. If the disk was encrypted and the key was stored somewhere else, the answer is almost certainly no. If the key was taped to the disk, the answer is yes. The same question applies to a network capture. A capture of encrypted traffic gives an attacker little beyond metadata, while a capture of cleartext hands over the payload. Encryption at rest and in transit is the thing that decides which of those two stories you tell.

Make the encryption decision for each fabric explicitly, based on whether tenants share it.

6. Availability: denial of service​

Figure 2.21.3. Each kind of denial of service exhausts a different resource and needs a different defence.

Picture a service that is healthy in every way except one: nobody can reach it. That is what a denial-of-service (DoS) attack does. It does not steal anything. It simply fills the shop with so many fake customers that real ones cannot get in. A distributed attack (DDoS) does the same thing with many sources at once. The table lists four classes, and each one needs a different defense.

TypeMechanismMitigation
VolumetricSaturate bandwidth (UDP floods, amplification via DNS/NTP/memcached reflection)Upstream scrubbing/anycast absorb, ISP/CDN protection, BCP38 anti-spoofing, RTBH
Protocol/state exhaustionSYN floods, connection table exhaustion, fragment attacksSYN cookies, connection limits, stateless filtering, larger tables, proxies
Application layerHTTP floods, slow-loris, expensive queries, API abuseRate limiting, WAF, caching, CAPTCHA/auth, request cost limits, autoscaling with caps
Control-planeFlood routers' CPUs with ICMP/ARP/routing packetsCoPP, ICMP rate limiting, routing protocol authentication

Volumetric attacks saturate bandwidth, so you absorb them upstream with scrubbing or anycast, and you stop spoofed sources with BCP38. Protocol attacks exhaust connection state, which SYN cookies and connection limits address. Application-layer attacks go after expensive requests, so rate limiting, a WAF, caching and request cost limits help. Control-plane floods hit the router CPU, so CoPP and routing authentication protect it.

The main techniques are rate limiting and policing, SYN cookies with sensible backlogs (chapter 2.6), and anycast to spread load across sites. Blackhole routing (RTBH) drops traffic bound for an attacked destination. It sacrifices the victim address to save the rest of the network. Selective BGP traffic diversion uses the NO_PEER and NO_ADVERTISE communities to steer traffic away from saturated peering links. BCP 38 and uRPF drop packets with spoofed sources, which prevents reflection. Traffic scrubbing services, overprovisioning and autoscaling with budget caps add capacity. Separating critical services, such as putting management on its own network, limits the damage. Finally, rehearse your runbooks before you need them.

Inference APIs are attractive targets, because each request can consume GPU seconds. That makes them a resource-exhaustion target. One expensive prompt costs far more than a web page, so a modest stream of them can starve a cluster. Use authentication, per-key quotas, token-based rate limits and admission control (chapter 2.20).

Walk through a SYN flood step by step, since the mechanism explains the defence. The attacker sends many connection requests, each with a fake source address. The server answers each one with a SYN-ACK and stores a half-open entry, which waits for a final acknowledgement that never arrives. The table of half-open entries fills, and legitimate clients are refused because no room is left. SYN cookies change the server's answer. The server encodes the connection details into the sequence number of its SYN-ACK and stores nothing. A real client returns the value, and the server rebuilds the connection from it. The attacker, who never sends the final acknowledgement, gains no stored state at all.

Set your rate limits and quotas before traffic spikes, not during an attack.

7. SSH in depth (and hardening)​

Picture a new engineer who logs in to a production server for the first time. The terminal asks whether to trust a key it has never seen, and the engineer types yes without reading the question. Later, a colleague's laptop shows a warning that the server's key has changed. Both moments are about trust, and SSH is built around making trust explicit. The paragraphs below explain the keys, the warnings, and the settings that make logins safer.

SSH is the tool you reach for on a Linux fleet. It gives you an encrypted shell, command execution, file transfer with scp and sftp, port forwarding and tunnelling (including X11), and key-based authentication. It is the de facto remote-administration protocol, and it replaced telnet and rlogin for good reason. Infrastructure engineers cannot skip understanding it, and almost every cluster compromise story has an SSH key somewhere in it.

Public-key cryptography in a paragraph​

Symmetric ciphers need both sides to share one secret key. If someone steals that key, everything it protects falls with it, and the two sides also had to find a safe way to share it in the first place. Public-key schemes solve this with a key pair. You can share the public key freely. It is like an open padlock that anyone can snap shut, while only you hold the key that opens it. Only the holder of the private key can decrypt what was encrypted to the public key, or produce a signature that the public key verifies. SSH uses public keys in both directions. Host keys let the client verify the server, and user keys let the server verify the client. Both sides then agree on a symmetric session key for the bulk of the traffic. The private key must never be exposed.

Host keys and trust​

Every server has host key pairs. The private halves live at /etc/ssh/ssh_host_ed25519_key, _rsa_key and _ecdsa_key, and the .pub files hold the public parts. The install process creates them with ssh-keygen -A. On your first connection, the client receives the public key of the server and stores it in ~/.ssh/known_hosts. This is called trust on first use. On later connections, a mismatch triggers the "REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED" warning. That warning can be harmless, for example after a reinstall, a replaced cloud instance or regenerated keys. It can also be a man-in-the-middle attack, so the warning alone does not tell you which one you face. A man-in-the-middle is someone who sits between you and the real server and pretends to be each to the other.

Verify the new fingerprint through a second channel before you trust it. Use ssh-keygen -lf, the cloud serial console output, or a configuration-managed known_hosts file. Then remove the stale line with ssh-keygen -R host. When you replace a machine, copy the old host keys so that users see no mismatch. In clouds, publish host keys through metadata, or use SSH host certificates, covered below.

Prefer ed25519 keys. Use ECDSA or RSA of at least 3072 bits only where you must. Disable DSA, which is obsolete.

Where SSH falls short​

SSH has one weak spot, and it comes at the start. Before you connect, your client needs the public host key of the server, and you do not always receive that key through a safe channel. You can compare fingerprints by hand so that a spoofed server does not fool you, or you can use SSH host certificates, which the later sections describe.

Client​

The block below shows the everyday client commands. The comments show the flags that change the behaviour of each command.

ssh user@host                          # login ; use -p for another port ; -i key ; -J jump
ssh user@host 'uptime'                 # run a command
tar zcf - dir | ssh host 'tar zxf -'   # pipeline over SSH
scp file user@host:/path ; sftp user@host ; rsync -a -e ssh dir/ host:/dest/
ssh -L 8888:localhost:8888 user@gpu01  # local forward (e.g. Jupyter on a GPU node): browse http://localhost:8888
ssh -R 2222:localhost:22 user@pub      # remote forward ; -D 1080 SOCKS proxy
ssh -J bastion.example.com user@10.0.0.45        # ProxyJump through a bastion
ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -a 100 -C "alice@laptop"    # create a key (use a passphrase)
ssh-copy-id -i ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub user@host    # install public key into authorized_keys
ssh-add ~/.ssh/id_ed25519 ; ssh-add -l            # use ssh-agent so the passphrase is entered once

The first line opens a login shell, and the next runs one command remotely. The pipeline streams a directory across in a single connection. The forwarding examples are the ones to remember. The -L line lets you open a Jupyter notebook on a GPU node in your local browser, and -J jumps through a bastion. Next, the ~/.ssh/config example shows how to avoid typing the bastion route every time:

Host bastion
  HostName bastion.example.com
  User alice
  IdentityFile ~/.ssh/id_ed25519
Host gpu-*
  ProxyJump bastion
  User alice
  ControlMaster auto
  ControlPath ~/.ssh/cm-%r@%h:%p
  ControlPersist 10m
  ServerAliveInterval 30

This config sends every gpu-* host through the bastion. It also reuses one connection, which ControlPersist 10m keeps open for 10 minutes.

Server (sshd_config) hardening​

Remember that sshd_config configures the server, while ssh_config configures the client. Verify each directive with man sshd_config. Apply them through /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/*.conf, and test with sshd -t while a second session stays open. That second session is your lifeline: if the new configuration locks you out, the old session lets you fix it. The key directives look like this:

Port 22
PermitRootLogin no                 # or prohibit-password for break-glass
PasswordAuthentication no          # keys (or certificates) only
KbdInteractiveAuthentication no
PubkeyAuthentication yes
AuthenticationMethods publickey    # or publickey,keyboard-interactive for MFA
AllowGroups ssh-users              # explicit allow-list
MaxAuthTries 3
LoginGraceTime 20
X11Forwarding no
AllowAgentForwarding no            # agent forwarding lets a compromised host use your keys
AllowTcpForwarding no              # enable selectively per Match block
ClientAliveInterval 300
ClientAliveCountMax 2
LogLevel VERBOSE                   # logs key fingerprints used
HostKey /etc/ssh/ssh_host_ed25519_key
KexAlgorithms ...                  # use the distro's modern defaults; only remove weak legacy items

Restrict SSH by network. Allow it only from bastions and management networks, using security groups, nftables or ListenAddress. Do not expose it to the Internet unless you must. If you do expose it, rate limit it and run fail2ban.

A bastion, or jump host, gives you one audited entry point with MFA and session logging. Use ProxyJump rather than agent forwarding, so your keys never sit on the intermediate host.

SSH certificates remove most of the per-key work. A trusted SSH CA signs short-lived user and host certificates, using a command such as ssh-keygen -s ca -I id -n user -V +1d key.pub. Servers trust the CA through TrustedUserCAKeys, and clients trust host certificates through @cert-authority in known_hosts. That ends per-key distribution and trust on first use. Clusters often adopt this pattern with tools such as HashiCorp Vault SSH, Smallstep step-ca, Teleport, or cloud-native OS Login and IAP.

Rotate and revoke keys with RevokedKeys. Prefer hardware-backed keys, such as FIDO2 ed25519-sk or a YubiKey. Protect private keys with passphrases and agent timeouts, and audit your authorized_keys files. Manage sshd_config, known_hosts and authorised keys through configuration management, and watch logins with last, journalctl -u ssh, auditd or central logs.

The sshd daemon is a prime target. Keep OpenSSH updated, limit the versions and algorithms it accepts, and consider putting it behind a VPN or a zero-trust proxy. A standalone sshd is the norm. Socket-activated sshd (ssh.socket) can delay first logins while keys are generated, so handle the systemd units according to your distribution.

fail2ban​

On an Internet-facing host, SSH brute-force attempts never stop. Within minutes of a server getting a public address, robots start guessing passwords. fail2ban watches the logs, either journald or /var/log/auth.log. After a set number of failures from one address within a window, it adds a firewall rule that bans that address for a period, then removes the rule. You configure jails in /etc/fail2ban/jail.d/*.local, with [sshd] enabled = true, maxretry, findtime and bantime. Prefer nftables actions. fail2ban cuts log noise and CPU load, but it does not replace keys-only logins or network restriction. It only slows a brute-force attack, and a shared NAT address can lock out legitimate users. Check its state with fail2ban-client status sshd.

Legacy services​

inetd/xinetd were superservers that listened on many ports and spawned handlers. TCP wrappers (/etc/hosts.allow, /etc/hosts.deny, tcpd) controlled access to them. Systemd socket units and firewalls have largely replaced both, but you will still meet them on old systems. Avoid exposing telnetd, rlogind, rexecd and FTP, because they send credentials in cleartext and have chronic vulnerabilities. Replace them with SSH, SFTP or rsync. If a cleartext service cannot be replaced, wrap it in TLS with Stunnel.

Replace cleartext services with SSH, SFTP or rsync wherever you can.

8. Scanning and auditing exposure​

Before an attacker finds an open port, you should find it yourself. An attacker's first move is nearly always a scan, and you can run the same scan first. nmap discovers hosts and ports, and it fingerprints the services behind them.

nmap -sn 10.0.0.0/24                  # host discovery only (ping/ARP sweep)
nmap -p- -T4 10.0.0.45                # all TCP ports
nmap -sV -O 10.0.0.45                 # service versions, OS guess
nmap -sU --top-ports 50 10.0.0.45     # common UDP ports
nmap -6 fe80::1%eth0                  # IPv6
nmap --script ssl-enum-ciphers -p 443 host     # TLS protocol/cipher audit
ss -tulpn                             # compare with what the host itself says it listens on

Picture an engineer who runs nmap from a laptop on the office network and sees only the SSH port on a server. The result looks reassuring. A scan from the bastion, which sits on a different segment, shows a second port that the office laptop never reached. The firewall was allowing that second port only from the bastion, so the first scan missed it. Both scans were correct, and each one answered a different question about the same server. That is why the scan has to come from more than one place before you trust it.

Run scans from at least two vantage points, the host itself and another network, ideally one outside your perimeter. That shows what the firewall really blocks. What a server believes about its own ports and what the outside world sees can be very different, and the gap is exactly where surprises hide. Scan only networks you own or have written permission to test. Scans get logged, they can disrupt fragile devices, and they may break policy or the law.

Add vulnerability scanners such as OpenVAS, Nessus and Trivy for images. Add configuration baselines such as CIS benchmarks and Lynis, and keep a continuous asset inventory. The rpcbind service on port 111, along with NFS and NIS, is a common unwanted exposure, so disable it if you do not use it. Typical findings are forgotten dev servers, default credentials and management ports exposed beyond need. Watch for BMC on 623 and 443, Redis on 6379, the Docker API on 2375, and kubelet on 10250. Also watch etcd on 2379, Jupyter on 8888, TensorBoard on 6006, Triton on 8000-8002, the Ray dashboard on 8265, and MLflow on 5000.

Scan from outside, compare the result with ss, and close every port you cannot justify.

9. Basic rules and how attacks reach AI clusters​

Most intrusions into a GPU cluster start with something small and ordinary. A forgotten dashboard, a stolen cloud key or an unpatched service gives the attacker a foothold. The rules below shrink that exposure, and the table shows the paths attackers use in practice.

Picture a data scientist who starts a Jupyter notebook on a GPU node to test a model. The notebook listens on all interfaces, with a token that the scientist copied into a chat window. A week later the notebook is still running, the token is still valid, and a scanner finds the port open from outside the cluster. Nothing was broken on purpose, but the open service was the attacker's way in. A simple habit prevents this: bind the notebook to localhost, tunnel to it over SSH, and stop it when the work is done.

Run as few services as possible. Every running service is a door, and a door nobody uses is a door nobody watches. Block everything you do not need with a firewall, both on the host and at the perimeter. Track and patch every service you expose, and subscribe to security advisories. Use long-term support releases on servers, because security teams concentrate their work on stable, supported releases. Give accounts only to people who need them, because local access makes privilege escalation far easier than a remote attack. Verify signatures and hashes on every download, and install software only from sources you trust.

Attackers pursue three main outcomes. Full compromise means gaining root, through a service exploit or through a weak account combined with a privilege-escalation bug. Denial of service takes the service away, as section 6 describes. Malware is the third outcome, and on GPU clusters it is mostly cryptominers and botnet agents. ASLR, stack protections, least privilege, SELinux or AppArmor, and containers with seccomp make compromise harder.

10. Securing routing and the control plane​

Strong data-plane security does not help if someone can hijack the control plane. Routing protocols decide where traffic goes, so they need protection of their own. If a stranger can whisper a better route into your router, your carefully filtered traffic will happily flow through the stranger's machine. Start by authenticating them. OSPF and IS-IS use HMAC-SHA, and BGP uses TCP-AO or MD5. For eBGP, use GTSM with TTL 255. GTSM is a trick that accepts a BGP packet only if its TTL is still at the maximum, which proves the sender is directly attached, because every router in between would have lowered it. Restrict BGP and OSPF to specific interfaces and peers, and make passive-interface the default.

Picture a peer that announces, one afternoon, a set of routes it has never announced before. Its prefixes overlap with a block of your own addresses, and the new routes are more specific, so your routers prefer them. Traffic meant for your servers starts flowing to the peer. Without a prefix list, a max-prefix limit or RPKI origin validation, nothing stops that announcement. With them, the routes are rejected at the edge, and the traffic keeps flowing to your own network. The check happens on the way in, which is the only moment you can stop a leak before it spreads.

Filter the routes that come in. On every peer, apply prefix lists and max-prefix limits, and use RPKI origin validation. Reject bogons, default routes from the wrong peer and overly specific prefixes. Make sure you never carry transit traffic by accident.

Control-plane policing (CoPP) and hardware rate limits protect the CPU from traffic punted to it, such as ARP, ICMP, routing, SSH, SNMP and NTP. Punted traffic is traffic that the forwarding hardware hands up to the slow, general-purpose processor because it is addressed to the router itself. Restrict management protocols with ACLs. Disable the services you do not use on network devices. That includes HTTP management, SNMPv1 and SNMPv2c, CDP and LLDP on untrusted ports, IP source routing and redirects.

Stop spoofing at the edge. Apply BCP 38 ingress filtering, and use uRPF in strict mode at edges and loose mode on transit. Add DHCP snooping and IP source guard to the same design.

Put management on an out-of-band network, reached through jump hosts. Use SSH, NETCONF or gNMI with strong authentication, AAA with TACACS+, config backups with diffs, signed images and secure boot where the platform supports it.

Route every change through automation with review (chapters 2.28 and 2.29), and watch for configuration drift.

Decide which of these controls you will enforce before you add a new BGP peer.

12. Zero trust and segmentation​

Figure 2.21.5. A default-deny policy plus one allow rule lets only the gateway reach the Triton pod on TCP 8001.

Zero trust replaces the old rule that anything inside the perimeter is trusted. The old rule resembled an office where the receptionist checks you at the front door and, after that, you can walk into any room. Instead, you authenticate and authorise every request based on identity, device posture and context. You assume a breach has already happened. You apply least privilege, encrypt everywhere, and verify continuously while you log. Four building blocks make that practical.

Identity-aware access comes first. Identity-aware proxies, ZTNA, Teleport, Tailscale or WireGuard meshes, and cloud IAP replace broad VPNs. Service identity comes next. Services use mTLS and short-lived certificates, issued through SPIFFE or SPIRE, a service mesh or cert-manager (chapter 2.10).

Microsegmentation restricts east-west traffic to what each workload needs. In Kubernetes, NetworkPolicy gives you default-deny ingress and egress per namespace, then allow rules by label. Calico and Cilium add L3, L4 and L7 policies, DNS-based egress and eBPF enforcement. In the fabric, VRFs, VXLAN tenants and GBP do the same job, which chapters 2.16 and 2.17 cover. Cloud security groups and firewall tags finish the picture.

Policy as code ties it together. Keep policies under version control, test them in CI, deploy them through GitOps, and watch for drift.

The manifest below applies that pattern to an inference namespace. It starts by denying all traffic, then allows only the gateway to reach the Triton model server.

# Kubernetes: default deny, then allow only the inference gateway to reach the model server
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: default-deny, namespace: inference }
spec: { podSelector: {}, policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress] }
---
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: allow-gateway-to-triton, namespace: inference }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: triton } }
  policyTypes: [Ingress]
  ingress:
  - from: [ { podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: gateway } } } ]
    ports: [ { protocol: TCP, port: 8001 } ]

Only the gateway may reach the Triton pod, and only on TCP port 8001. Remember to allow DNS egress and metrics scraping when you enforce default-deny egress. Then test enforcement, because a policy does nothing unless the CNI implements it.

Test enforcement before you rely on the default-deny policy, because a policy does nothing unless the CNI enforces it.

13. Security specifics for AI clusters​

Picture a bare-metal GPU cluster that several tenants share. Their traffic crosses shared switches, and each node has a management controller. The sections below follow the attack paths from the management controller outward.

Picture a bare-metal GPU server that a tenant has rented for a week. When the tenant returns the machine, the provider reinstalls the operating system and assumes the box is clean. The BMC still holds the tenant's settings, and it may still accept the old password. Anyone who reaches that BMC can reinstall firmware or read memory, whatever the operating system does. The lesson for the provider is that the BMC needs its own credential rotation and its own firmware check at every handover, not just a fresh operating system.

Start with the BMC. Out-of-band management, through IPMI, Redfish, iDRAC, iLO, XClarity or the DGX BMC, has a weak history. Default credentials, the old IPMI 2.0 cipher-zero, exposed web UIs and unauthenticated firmware vectors have all caused trouble. Put BMCs on an isolated management network with strict ACLs. Give each one a unique, strong credential, and disable IPMI-over-LAN if you do not use it. Keep firmware current, serve HTTPS with real certificates, and allow access only through jump hosts. Watch for Redfish events and firmware changes.

Next comes provisioning. PXE, DHCP, TFTP and image servers are trust anchors for every node. A node that boots from the network believes whatever the image server tells it, so whoever controls that server controls the fleet. Protect them with signed images, HTTPS boot, secure boot and TPM measured boot where you use them. Isolate their VLAN, and treat a compromise there as a fleet-wide problem.

InfiniBand has its own keys. Partition keys (P_Key) segment the fabric per tenant, and the Subnet Manager programs them. M_Key and the SM key protect management, and Q_Key applies to datagram queue pairs. Secure the SM and UFM. RoCE tenancy relies on VRFs, VLANs and QoS isolation instead. GPU-to-GPU RDMA traffic is generally unencrypted, so physical and logical isolation both matter. NIC firmware and DPUs such as BlueField add isolation features, including ARM-side network policy and host isolation modes.

A worked example makes the P_Key format concrete. Take one port with P_Key 0x8042 and another with 0x0042. Bits 0 to 14 hold the Partition ID, and both ports share the ID 0x0042. The 0x8000 bit is the membership bit, and only 0x8042 has it set, so that port is a full member. Two ports that both show 0x0042 are limited members, and they cannot communicate with each other.

Isolation has to cover every layer: network (VRFs, VXLAN or IB partitions), compute (MIG, separate nodes or confidential computing), and storage (per-tenant namespaces and encryption keys). Schedulers such as Slurm/K8s enforce quotas, cgroups and user namespaces.

Inference APIs need authentication (API keys or OIDC), TLS, rate limits, quotas and request size limits. At the application layer, add controls for prompt injection and data exfiltration. Log requests without leaking sensitive prompts, add a WAF, and control egress for tool-calling agents.

Supply-chain controls start with image provenance. Sign images with cosign and verify them at admission. Pin base images, scan them with Trivy or Grype, and use curated model registries. Verify checksums of weights, and use private package mirrors. NVIDIA driver and Container Toolkit CVEs can lead to container escape, so patch them quickly and do not run privileged containers casually.

Keep secrets out of images and environment dumps. Use Kubernetes Secrets with encryption at rest, backed by an external store such as Vault or KMS, and issue short-lived credentials. Rotate tokens, and use workload identity in place of long-lived cloud keys on nodes.

Egress filtering from GPU nodes should allow only the package mirrors and registries on an allow-list. Watch DNS, keep flow logs, and alert on large outbound transfers.

Finally, monitor each node. Per-node anomaly detection can catch unexpected GPU processes, such as cryptominers. Collect audit logs from schedulers and the Kubernetes API, flow logs from fabric edges, and host IDS data from auditd and Falco. Send everything to a central SIEM, the system that gathers logs from everywhere so that one analyst can search them together.

Decide which tenants may share each fabric, and write that decision down before you put them on the same hardware.

14. The OODA loop for security operations​

Picture an alert at three in the morning that shows a GPU node sending unexpected traffic. The team has minutes to decide what to do, and the attacker is moving too. A structured loop helps, because it stops the team from reacting to the first noise and from waiting too long for certainty. The loop below is the one military pilots first used, and it fits security work well.

An alert fires, and the clock starts. Security work is a cycle you repeat, not a state you reach. Colonel John Boyd's OODA loop describes that cycle in four steps: Observe, Orient, Decide and Act. The side that completes the loop faster usually wins. Boyd first described it for fighter pilots, and it fits an incident well, because the attacker is also running a loop of their own.

  1. Observe: Collect telemetry such as flow records, logs, packet captures on demand, IDS alerts, authentication events, vulnerability scans and threat intelligence. Without observation there is no detection. Tune what you collect, because data overload hides the signal.
  2. Orient: Put the observations in context. Ask what is normal, which asset is affected, what the attacker is likely after (MITRE ATT&CK technique), and what the business impact would be. Orientation is where experience and a good inventory pay off, and bias and assumptions are its main enemies.
  3. Decide: Choose a response. You can contain (isolate a host or VLAN, block an IP, revoke credentials), eradicate, recover, or keep monitoring. Predefined playbooks speed up the decision.
  4. Act: Execute the response, preferably automatically. Then observe the result and loop again. Afterwards, improve your defenses through lessons learned and a post-incident review.

Walk through the three-in-the-morning alert from the top. Observe: the flow logs show a GPU node sending steady traffic to an address outside the company, and the traffic is far larger than the node's usual pattern. Orient: the team checks the asset inventory and finds that the node runs a training job with no reason to talk to that address. They also look up the address in the threat feed, and the feed lists it as a known command-and-control server. Decide: the playbook says to quarantine the node. Act: the node's port moves to a quarantine VLAN, the credentials it used are revoked, and the team starts a forensic copy of its disk. Then the loop starts again, to check that the traffic has stopped and that no other node talks to the same address.

Build fast loops on purpose. Automate isolation hooks, such as moving a port to a quarantine VLAN, applying a K8s deny policy or disabling an account. Write runbooks, rehearse them in game days, and assign clear ownership. Keep forensic readiness too. Preserve logs and memory, avoid destroying evidence, and record timelines.

Decide now which containment steps you can automate, because those are the steps that shorten your loop.

16. Common mistakes​

These mistakes show up on clusters again and again. Check your setup against them.

Start with SSH. Password logins on Internet-facing hosts, agent forwarding to untrusted hosts, and long-lived unprotected keys all cause trouble. So does StrictHostKeyChecking no everywhere.

Network layout and firewalls cause the next group. A flat network where workloads can reach management interfaces, or BMCs and dashboards left exposed, is a common mistake. So are "allow any" rules, missing egress filtering and rules nobody reviews.

Relying on the perimeter alone, or trusting east-west traffic implicitly, leaves the layers behind it unprotected. Treating TLS and mTLS as optional inside the cluster is another mistake. Without logs and time sync, you cannot reconstruct an incident. Delayed driver, runtime or CVE patches are one problem, and running privileged containers by default is another.

Scanning or testing a network without authorisation is the final mistake.

Checkpoint questions (20)​

1. Define Attack Surface, Vulnerability, Exploit, and Risk, and state the six categories of STRIDE.
  • Attack surface: every reachable entry point an attacker can interact with, such as open ports, APIs, BMCs, the supply chain and users.
  • Vulnerability: a weakness in design, implementation, configuration or operations.
  • Exploit: the specific tool or technique that uses a vulnerability.
  • Risk: likelihood times impact of an adverse event.
  • STRIDE: Spoofing identity, Tampering with data, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, and Elevation of privilege.
2. Why should biometrics not be the only remote authentication credential?

Biometrics, such as fingerprints, face scans and iris patterns, break two properties that a cryptographic secret needs. First, they are not secret. You leave fingerprints on surfaces and your face on cameras everywhere. Second, once a biometric is stored in a backend database and stolen, you cannot rotate it or revoke it. So use biometrics only to unlock a local hardware enclave, such as a TPM, a Secure Enclave or a FIDO2 key. That enclave then signs challenges with a private key you can rotate.

3. What are the three network security problem areas, and how does Defense in Depth work across them?

The three problem areas are access control (only authorised principals reach data), data confidentiality and integrity (in transit and at rest), and service availability (resisting DoS). Any single layer can be bypassed or misconfigured, and deep inspection at line rate is expensive. So defense in depth places overlapping controls at every boundary. For example, edge ACLs with RPKI and BCP38 come first, then a stateful firewall or WAF, then VRF, VLAN and NetworkPolicy segmentation, then mTLS and RBAC service authentication, and finally encryption at rest with continuous telemetry and exfiltration monitoring. An attacker must cross several independent layers, which gives defenders time to detect and contain the intrusion.

4. Compare RADIUS and TACACS+ in AAA (Authentication, Authorization, Accounting).
  • RADIUS (UDP ports 1812 and 1813, RFC 2865 and RFC 2866): built for network access authentication, such as 802.1X switch ports, WPA-Enterprise Wi-Fi and VPNs. It combines authentication and authorisation in one reply. It encrypts only the password attribute, so protect it with RadSec, TLS or IPsec.
  • TACACS+ (TCP port 49): built for network device administration, such as SSH into switches and routers. It keeps authentication, authorisation and accounting separate. It encrypts the entire packet body and supports per-command authorisation and audit logging. That lets you control exactly which CLI commands an engineer may run.
5. Compare a Stateless Packet Filter (ACL), a Stateful Firewall (conntrack), an NGFW / WAF, and Microsegmentation (NetworkPolicy).
  • Stateless ACL: checks each packet's headers (source and destination IP, protocol, L4 ports, and the TCP SYN and ACK flags) in switch TCAM at line rate. It keeps no connection memory.
  • Stateful firewall (nftables or iptables with ct state): tracks active connections in conntrack by their 5-tuples. It permits ESTABLISHED,RELATED return packets automatically and drops unsolicited inbound traffic.
  • NGFW and WAF: inspect Layer 7 payloads for SQL injection, SSRF, prompt and API abuse, and IPS signatures.
  • Microsegmentation (Kubernetes NetworkPolicy, Cilium eBPF, VXLAN GBP): enforces rules between individual pods and containers. It uses identity labels from the orchestrator, not static IP subnets.
6. Why is Egress Filtering just as important as Ingress Filtering on GPU cluster nodes?

If an attacker compromises a container or a dependency through a supply-chain CVE or remote code execution, the next steps are command-and-control (C2) callbacks, downloads of stage-2 payloads, and exfiltration of model weights or training data. Default-deny egress filtering stops those steps. GPU nodes can then reach only internal storage, internal DNS and an approved private artifact mirror. That breaks reverse shells and blocks data exfiltration.

7. Match the four DoS/DDoS classes to their primary mitigations, including BCP 38, RTBH, and NO_PEER / NO_ADVERTISE.
  • Volumetric floods (UDP reflection and amplification through DNS or NTP): BCP 38 and uRPF at the network edge, anycast with upstream scrubbing centres, Remote-Triggered Black Hole (RTBH, community 65535:666), and the BGP NO_PEER and NO_ADVERTISE communities to divert traffic off congested peering links.
  • Protocol and state exhaustion (TCP SYN floods and conntrack exhaustion): tcp_syncookies = 1, SYN rate limits, and stateless edge ACLs.
  • Application and GPU exhaustion (expensive LLM prompts): API authentication, per-tenant token-bucket rate limits, max_tokens caps and admission control.
  • Control-plane CPU floods (ARP, ICMP and BGP punts): Control-Plane Policing (CoPP), GTSM (TTL = 255), and TCP-AO or HMAC-SHA.
8. How does SSH authenticate both the server and the user, and what does the REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED warning mean?

First, the server proves its identity with its host key pair, such as /etc/ssh/ssh_host_ed25519_key. On the first connection, which is trust on first use (TOFU), the client records the server's public key in ~/.ssh/known_hosts. Next, over an ephemeral Diffie-Hellman encrypted channel, the user proves possession of a private key, such as ~/.ssh/id_ed25519. The server checks that proof against ~/.ssh/authorized_keys. If the server's host key ever differs from the one in ~/.ssh/known_hosts, SSH aborts with REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED. The server may have been reinstalled or re-keyed. An attacker may also be running a man-in-the-middle (MitM) attack. Verify the new fingerprint out of band, for example at the console with ssh-keygen -lf, before you run ssh-keygen -R <host>.

9. Why is AllowAgentForwarding yes dangerous when connecting through a bastion or jump host, and why should you use ProxyJump (ssh -J) instead?

With SSH agent forwarding (ssh -A), sshd creates a Unix domain socket on the intermediate host. That socket points back to the ssh-agent on your laptop. Anyone with root access on the intermediate host can hijack the socket and use your keys to log in to every other server you can reach. With ProxyJump (ssh -J bastion user@gpu01), SSH opens a TCP port forward through the bastion. The end-to-end SSH handshake then happens directly between your laptop and gpu01, so your private key and agent socket never reach the bastion.

10. How do SSH Certificates (ssh-keygen -s) solve known_hosts TOFU and authorized_keys sprawl across a 1,000-node GPU cluster?

Copying static public keys into ~/.ssh/authorized_keys on 1,000 nodes, and keeping 1,000 host keys in every engineer's known_hosts, does not scale. Certificates fix both problems. First, a host CA signs each node's host public key, which produces a HostCertificate. Engineers add one @cert-authority *.cluster.example.com <CA-pub-key> line to known_hosts, and then they trust any node the host CA signed, with no TOFU prompt. Second, a user CA, such as Vault, step-ca or Teleport, issues a short-lived user certificate after the engineer logs in with SSO and MFA. The example uses 8 hours, with -V +8h. Each node sets TrustedUserCAKeys /etc/ssh/user_ca.pub, so nodes need no per-user authorized_keys files, and access expires on its own.

11. List the eight essential sshd_config hardening directives for production Linux servers.

The eight are as follows. Start with PermitRootLogin no (or prohibit-password). Then add PasswordAuthentication no and KbdInteractiveAuthentication no, so only keys or certificates work. Third, PubkeyAuthentication yes with AuthenticationMethods publickey. Fourth, AllowGroups ssh-users, which gives you an explicit allow-list. Fifth, AllowAgentForwarding no. Sixth, X11Forwarding no and AllowTcpForwarding no, unless a Match block allows them. Seventh, MaxAuthTries 3 with LoginGraceTime 20. The eighth is LogLevel VERBOSE, which logs the key fingerprint that authenticated. Always test with sudo sshd -t before you reload, and keep an existing root session open.

12. What does fail2ban do, and why is it NOT a substitute for PasswordAuthentication no?

fail2ban tails /var/log/auth.log or journald. When an IP exceeds maxretry failed logins within findtime, it adds a temporary nftables or iptables drop rule. fail2ban cuts log spam and brute-force CPU load. It does not stop a distributed botnet, though. A botnet of 50,000 IPs can guess weak passwords at 2 attempts per IP without triggering a single ban. A shared corporate NAT IP can also lock out an entire office. That is why PasswordAuthentication no and network allow-listing remain mandatory.

13. Why should you compare local ss -tulpn output against an external nmap scan (nmap -p- -sV), and what AI and cluster ports are most frequently exposed by mistake?

ss -tulpn shows every socket bound on the host, such as 0.0.0.0:* and 127.0.0.1:*. An external nmap scan from another subnet or zone shows what really passes through the host and perimeter firewalls, so the difference between the two is the finding. Common high-risk exposures on AI clusters include an unauthenticated Docker daemon on 2375, and kubelet on 10250 with etcd on 2379. The Ray dashboard on 8265 allows arbitrary job submission. Jupyter Notebook on 8888, Triton on 8000–8002 and MLflow on 5000 are often left open. Redis on 6379, rpcbind and NFS on 111 and 2049, and BMC IPMI on UDP 623 with Redfish on 443 complete the list.

14. What are the seven classic Layer-2 LAN attacks and their corresponding switch defenses?

MAC table flooding is stopped by port security, which limits the MACs per port, and by storm control. A rogue DHCP server is stopped by DHCP snooping, which drops DHCPOFFER and DHCPACK on untrusted ports, together with IP Source Guard. ARP spoofing and poisoning are stopped by Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), which checks ARP packets against the DHCP snooping binding table. An IPv6 rogue router advertisement is stopped by IPv6 RA Guard and ND inspection. A rogue switch or STP root hijack is stopped by BPDU Guard and Root Guard. VLAN hopping through double tagging or DTP is stopped by disabling DTP, setting the native VLAN to an unused ID, and pruning the trunk VLANs. Physical link wiretapping is stopped by IEEE 802.1AE MACsec line-rate encryption.

15. Define Zero Trust (NIST SP 800-207) and explain how Kubernetes NetworkPolicy enforces east-west microsegmentation.

Zero trust removes implicit trust based on network location. Being inside the cluster VLAN does not make a request trusted. Every request between users, devices and workloads must be strongly authenticated, for example with mTLS or SPIFFE, then authorised by least-privilege policy and encrypted. In Kubernetes, pods can talk to every other pod by default. A namespace-wide default-deny NetworkPolicy (podSelector: {}, policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress]) blocks all unsolicited east-west traffic. You then add label-based allow rules. For example, only app: gateway may reach app: triton on TCP 8001, plus DNS egress on 53. The CNI, such as Cilium or Calico, must enforce those rules.

16. Why are out-of-band BMCs (IPMI / Redfish) and the PXE provisioning network the highest-value targets in a bare-metal GPU cluster, and how do you secure them?

The BMC has total hardware control over the server, whatever OS runs on the host. It can mount virtual media, read console memory, reflash BIOS or BMC firmware, and reboot the machine. BMCs also have a long history of critical vulnerabilities, including IPMI 2.0 RAKP hash disclosure and Cipher 0 authentication bypass. Compromising the PXE, DHCP or HTTP provisioning server is just as serious, because it compromises the boot image of every node in the fleet. To secure both, place BMCs on a strictly air-gapped OOB management VLAN or VRF that only hardened jump hosts can reach. Disable legacy IPMI-over-LAN (UDP 623) and use authenticated Redfish over HTTPS (TLS). Set a unique password for each BMC, patch BMC firmware, and enforce UEFI Secure Boot with signed OS images.

17. How does InfiniBand partitioning (P_Key) isolate tenants in hardware, and how do Full (0x8000) and Limited (0x0000) memberships work?

The Subnet Manager (OpenSM or UFM) programs a table of 16-bit Partition Keys (P_Keys) into every Host Channel Adapter (HCA) port. M_Key protects management SMPs. Each key has two parts:

  • Bits 0 to 14 (15 bits) hold the Partition ID, from 0x0001 to 0x7FFF. The default partition is 0x7FFF.
  • Bit 15, the most significant bit (MSB, 0x8000), is the membership bit. A 1 means full member, and a 0 means limited member.

Every packet carries its P_Key in the Base Transport Header (BTH). The HCA silicon lets two ports communicate only if they share the exact same 15-bit Partition ID and at least one of them is a full member. Several untrusted tenants can therefore be limited members of a shared storage partition. They can reach a full-member storage server, while the hardware blocks them from reaching each other.

18. How do NVIDIA BlueField DPUs strengthen multi-tenant security on bare-metal GPU servers?

When a tenant has root access on a bare-metal GPU host, that tenant can disable any agent, iptables rule or CNI running inside the host kernel. A BlueField DPU runs its own Linux OS and a hardware-offloaded firewall pipeline built from Open vSwitch, eBPF, IPsec and RDMA. That pipeline sits on a separate Arm complex in front of the physical network ports, in host-isolated (zero-trust) mode. It enforces tenant VRF and EVPN isolation, firewall ACLs, storage authentication and line-rate encryption, all outside the tenant's reach.

19. What are the unique security threats to LLM inference APIs and AI supply chains?
  • Inference APIs: GPU resource-exhaustion DoS through long-context prompts, unauthorised model or weight extraction, prompt injection, and SSRF and data exfiltration through tool-calling agents. Counter these with strict egress allow-lists on agent sandboxes, per-key token quotas and prompt-redacted logging.
  • AI supply chain: malicious pickle weights, where torch.load can run arbitrary code. Prefer safetensors plus SHA-256 signature verification. Unverified container images are a risk too, so sign them with cosign and enforce the signatures in Kubernetes admission. Container-escape CVEs in GPU drivers and nvidia-container-toolkit matter as well. Patch them promptly, and avoid --privileged.
20. Explain Colonel John Boyd's OODA loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) as a network security operations model.

Colonel John Boyd of the USAF first developed the OODA loop for air combat. It models security operations as a race of decision cycles against the attacker.

  1. Observe: collect high-signal telemetry, such as NetFlow or IPFIX, DNS logs, auditd and Falco alerts for GPU processes, SSH and API auth logs, and BGP and RPKI monitors, without drowning in noise.
  2. Orient: correlate what you saw with the asset inventory, baseline behaviour and threat models (MITRE ATT&CK). Get past bias and ask whether this is real and what the blast radius is.
  3. Decide: pick a rehearsed playbook, such as quarantining a VLAN or applying a NetworkPolicy, revoking an SSH or SPIFFE certificate, or triggering a BGP RTBH.
  4. Act: contain the threat, ideally automatically. Then observe the effect, and feed the lessons back into hardening.
