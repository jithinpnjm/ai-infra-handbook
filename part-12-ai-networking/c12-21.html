Why this matters for AI infrastructure​

Picture a training job that was fast last week and now crawls. The GPUs sit idle for long stretches, the compute dashboards look normal, and nobody sees an error. The cause is often the network, but "the network" is not one thing. An AI factory runs three distinct fabrics. Each one has its own latency target, bandwidth scale, and failure boundary. Think of the three fabrics as three different roads: a neighborhood street, a regional highway, and a racetrack inside a pit lane. Each road has its own speed limit and its own pattern of accidents, so a traffic plan that works on one road can jam the others. Treat them as interchangeable byte pipes and you will see checkpoint stalls, packet-retransmission storms, or sub-microsecond tensor starvation, and you will not know which fabric to blame. The first step in any investigation is to name the fabric that owns the symptom.

  1. AI storage networks. Curated training datasets must stream continuously into GPU memory. Multi-terabyte checkpoints must also flush to flash without pausing the training steps. A local PCIe drive responds in roughly a hundred nanoseconds. That speed is excellent, but it only reaches the one server that holds the drive, which is the limit the next sentences describe. But a drive inside one server cannot hold a petabyte-scale corpus, and it cannot give thousands of nodes a shared namespace. Dedicated storage fabrics bridge that gap. They connect compute servers to shared flash arrays using NVMe-o-TCP, NVMe-o-RDMA over RoCEv2, and parallel file systems with GPUDirect Storage.   2. Ultra Ethernet Consortium scale-out fabrics. Production RoCEv2 clusters have grown past 100,000 GPUs. Even so, RoCEv2 still inherits legacy InfiniBand Queue Pair semantics and static flow hashing. It also suffers from Go-Back-N retransmission amplification and fragile PFC pause tuning.

    The Ultra Ethernet work starts from a simple complaint. RoCEv2 was built by stretching an older design onto Ethernet, and each stretch shows at scale. The rest of this bullet explains what the new specification changes, and it names the parts that matter most for a storage or training fabric.

UEC Specification v1.0 replaces the RoCEv2 transport stack with Ultra Ethernet Transport over UDP or native IP. The work is split between a Packet Delivery Sublayer and a Semantic Sublayer that is driven by Libfabric. UEC adds four packet delivery modes, built-in AES-GCM security, and both sender and receiver congestion control. It also brings link-level Credit-Based Flow Control, DSCP packet trimming, Link Layer Reliability, and In-Network Collectives.

  1. Scale-up super-accelerator fabrics. Tensor parallelism, Mixture-of-Experts token routing, multi-step reasoning, and diffusion micro-batching exchange activations at a sub-microsecond cadence. That cadence overwhelms PCIe and scale-out NICs. Proprietary scale-up fabrics such as NVIDIA NVLink 5 tie the buyer to one vendor. Two open standards have emerged to break that lock-in: the Open Compute Project Scale-Up Ethernet Transport under ESUN, and the Ultra Accelerator Link.

Each of the three domains answers a different question, so a single design rule will not fit all of them. Decide which domain a new cluster needs first, because each one has its own latency, bandwidth, and failure rules. A storage plan that borrows the training fabric's rules will look fine on paper and fail under a checkpoint burst.

1. AI Storage Network Lifecycle, Topologies, and Access Models​

1.1 The Four-Stage AI Data Lifecycle and Storage Access Tiers​

Think about a run that streams data well for days and then stalls every time it saves a checkpoint. A storage network built only for sequential dataset reads has no room for that write burst. Synchronous checkpoints fill the write buffers, and thousands of GPUs sit idle while they wait. Every large language model or multimodal pipeline passes through four data-lifecycle phases. Think of them as the stages of a factory line, where raw material enters at one end and finished parts leave at the other. Each station needs its own tools, and a jam at one station slows the whole line. Each phase has its own I/O sizes, concurrency patterns, and latency budgets. As you read the phases, notice that each one stresses storage in a different way. Ingestion is a steady stream, checkpointing is a sudden flood, and retrieval is a scatter of small reads. A single storage design has to survive all three in the same week.

  1. Data ingestion: Raw structured and unstructured datasets are pulled from external repositories, web crawls, or enterprise lakes into high-capacity staging pools.
  2. Data processing and curation: Tokenization, deduplication, quality filtering, and format conversion turn raw assets into training shards. This phase needs high sequential and random read and write throughput across distributed workers.
  3. AI model training and checkpointing: Training servers stream curated batches into GPU HBM over dedicated 100 Gbps to 200 Gbps storage NICs. Converged pods use 400 Gbps to 800 Gbps NICs instead. Servers also pause compute from time to time to flush multi-terabyte checkpoints of weights and optimizer states to flash arrays.
  4. Post-training data warehouse, archival, and RAG inference: Trained checkpoints, evaluation artifacts, and vector corpora move into long-term repositories and feeds for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. These workloads need low-latency random reads and continuous live updates.

To serve these four phases, operators combine three logical storage architectures: Object, Block, and File. Their protocol and fabric characteristics are summarized below. 1.2 Four Storage Fabric Topologies: Physical Fab A/B, Logical Fab A/B, Collapsed Spine, and Hybrid Cloud​

A single top-of-rack switch failure on a non-redundant storage network drops every active mount, and the training jobs on it crash. The failure is not dramatic in the logs, which is why the redundancy has to be designed in before anything breaks. Storage availability therefore depends on path diversity across Fabric A and Fabric B. Two paths that share one power feed or one rack switch are not really two paths, so check what each path depends on, not only its name. If Fabric A fails or goes down for maintenance, Fabric B must carry 100% of the storage workload. It cannot lose IOPS, bandwidth, or latency while it does that. Picture a bridge that carries two lanes of traffic. When one lane closes, the other lane has to carry the full load, or the jam spreads back to every car on the road. Backend GPU training fabrics need strict 1:1 non-blocking bandwidth. Picture a road where every car can keep moving at full speed at the same moment, with no merge that slows anyone down. Training traffic is that kind of road, because every GPU sends at the same time in a synchronized step. You can see why a storage design differs from a training design. A training fabric is built for a short burst of perfectly timed traffic, while a storage fabric must stay calm through many uneven requests over a long day. Dedicated storage leaf-spine fabrics can run at 1:3 oversubscription instead, because storage flows are shorter and less synchronized than AllReduce collectives. A storage read is a single conversation between a client and an array, so the traffic arrives in waves rather than all at once.

1.3 PCIe-Attached Drives vs Network-Attached Storage: Latency and When Each Fits​

Every GPU server already has drives, so you might ask why a separate storage network is needed at all. The answer starts inside the server. Local drives sit on the PCIe bus, the same internal bus that carries the CPU and the network card. That layout works well for one machine. It cannot share one dataset across many servers at the same time.

Picture two ways to hand a library to a team of readers. One way puts a copy of every book on every desk. The other keeps one shared shelf in the hall that everyone walks to. The two figures below show those two layouts for storage inside a server.

Figure 9-1, labeled PCIe-connected SSD/HDD, shows this first design. A horizontal bar stands for the PCIe bus, which is peripheral component interconnect express. The central processing unit sits above the bus, and a network interface card sits below it. A line runs from that NIC to the data center local area network. On the right side of the bus, four cylindrical storage devices are labeled local hard disk drive or solid state drive. Every component connects to the bus, which shows that networking and storage share one internal connection inside the server.

Figure 9-2, labeled network-connected SSD/HDD storage, shows the second design. A dedicated storage network interface card plugs into the PCIe bus and reaches a separate storage network. That network connects to disk arrays. In both designs, the server keeps a central processing unit and a LAN data center NIC as standard parts. Think of the LAN NIC as the front door that serves users and the cluster's management tools. The storage path is the loading dock beside it, and the two should not share the same door. That LAN is the frontend part of an AI data center.

From the application's point of view, reads and writes work the same way in both designs. The difference is distance. A PCIe-attached SSD reaches its local array in about 100 nanoseconds on generation 5. A network-attached array adds about 300 nanoseconds through an InfiniBand switch, or about 800 nanoseconds through an Ethernet switch. When you compare the two, the application sees the same reads and writes, so only the timing changes. That is good news for software, because a storage change does not require a code change, only a new set of timings to plan around.

Those few hundred nanoseconds matter less than they seem. When the network is only one to three hops away, and each switch adds only nanoseconds, IOPS can come close to PCIe-attached storage. A port-to-port switch latency of 700 to 900 nanoseconds still delivers high bus bandwidth for reads and writes. Latency and bandwidth are different measures. A long pipe can still carry a lot of water, even when the first drop takes a moment to arrive.

In AI training, both types are often used together. A typical pattern keeps hot data on local flash for speed and uses the shared file system for the copy that every node must see. Think of the local drive as a personal notebook and the shared file system as the team's common binder. Local storage can run a file system that is backed by an NVMe-oF system. Parallel file systems let many nodes share the same files, and they keep extra metadata. That metadata helps during training and when regular snapshots of a training job are taken. If you run both, you need to know where each file lives.

Putting the front-end LAN to work for storage would cut infrastructure cost. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies notes that AI data centers rarely do this. The reason is practical: a storage burst on the same LAN can slow the users and the services that share it. They deploy dedicated front-end and backend storage systems and storage networks instead. Keep the LAN for users, and give training data its own network.

1.4 Checkpoints and Fast Writes During Training​

Training is the phase where storage most often becomes the bottleneck. It follows the data preparation phase, and it is the stage where you must save and restore model checkpoints. A checkpoint is a snapshot of the model's weights and optimizer state, so a restore brings the training run back to a known point after a failure. Those saves and restores have to be designed on purpose. A checkpoint that nobody planned for tends to land in the middle of the busiest hour, and then it costs the most. A good checkpoint system protects the training investment already made, and it keeps GPUs free for new jobs.

Picture a checkpoint save that pauses every GPU in a large job while the write finishes. Each second of that pause is a second the cluster is paid for and not training. That is why the storage path has to be designed as carefully as the compute path.

Storage work should not steal time from the GPUs. As you plan a cluster, keep the storage path off the GPU's critical path. A dedicated data storage system keeps those storage tasks off the GPU path. An efficient storage system in the backend network offers quicker access, so it shortens the time needed to save checkpoint data. Shorter saves reduce how often training pauses and how long each pause lasts. Pauses hold back progress, so keep their frequency and duration low. A short save every hour is easier to live with than a long save every ten minutes, even when both add up to the same total time. Fast-write storage is therefore a core part of cluster efficiency, not an extra. A slow save is a tax that the whole job pays every time it runs, and a fast save is a rebate that returns that time to the GPUs.

1.5 Redundancy, Path Diversity, and Inter-Site Replication​

Suppose a switch in the storage rack reboots in the middle of a checkpoint. If the design has only one path, the save fails and the job may need to roll back. If the design has two paths, the save continues on the other path, and nobody notices the reboot.

Storage needs the same care as the compute network. Each storage network must offer node and link redundancy. Active storage A and backup storage B should form two paths to the same data. If path A fails, path B must carry the full load without losing IOPS, bandwidth, or latency. This is called path diversity. Think of it as having two roads to the same town, so a closed road does not cut the town off. When you audit a design, check each path on its own. Ask what happens to the load when that path is gone, and whether the other path can carry it without losing any of the three measures.

Replication across sites follows the same rule. A copy in a second building is only useful if it is current, and a copy that lags behind the primary can lose the last hours of training. Plan the replication lag as carefully as the path itself. Both storage domains need local site-level redundancy, plus inter-site replication after each read or write. Inter-site traffic often uses a different transport. When Fibre Channel storage runs inside a site, Fibre Channel over IP, or FCoIP, is a common choice for replication. Dedicated site-to-site replication gateways still play a role.

When a design uses NFS instead of a SAN, one disaster recovery design can cover both the application and the storage. A SAN is a separate block network, so it usually needs its own recovery plan, while an NFS share travels with the same network that the applications already use. Inter-site data redundancy can then ride on the same data center interconnect link as the applications. If the site already runs RoCEv2 for storage, inter-site RDMA is the usual replication method. Lossless inter-site RDMA is rare, though. If you plan that link, budget time for the extra care it needs. Keeping DCQCN working over a link of 40 km or more is hard. The control loop depends on feedback that arrives quickly, and a long link delays that feedback until the reaction comes too late to help.

Long-distance replication is harder than local replication because every extra kilometer adds delay. Lossless transports depend on tight timing, so they become fragile over long links. A transport that tolerates loss and delay is easier to keep running across a city or a region.

NVMe over TCP takes a different path for long-distance replication. It can run on a lossy IP core network. It does not engage DCQCN pushback mechanisms such as Priority Flow Control, or PFC. Instead, it adapts to network conditions. It uses reliable transport, and its maximum segment size, or MSS, requirements are more adaptable and less restrictive than those of the training cluster storage. That is why many teams choose it for distant copies.

1.6 Logical Fab A/B With EVPN-VXLAN Overlays​

Imagine a building with two sets of elevators that serve the same floors. If one set stops, the other set keeps people moving, but the building pays for both. The physical split works the same way, which is why the logical version exists.

Physical separation uses two independent sets of switches, and the cost is high. Logical separation keeps one physical fabric and splits it into two paths. In a physical design, each ToR switch connects to only NIC A or only NIC B. In a logical design, leaves 1 and 2 carry separate dark and light gray fabric connections. The storage SSD controllers follow the same split, either physically or logically. When you draw the design, show both versions on one page so the difference is visible. A reviewer who sees the two drawings side by side can spot a shared failure point in seconds, while a single drawing hides it.

Logical separation usually runs an EVPN-VXLAN overlay. An overlay is a set of tunnels that ride on top of the existing physical network, so each tunnel behaves like its own private road even though the cars share the same pavement. Fabric A and fabric B control traffic can use two separate RT5 EVPN instances. Think of each instance as a separate routing table that lives inside the shared fabric. Each table knows only its own routes, so traffic on one path does not leak into the other. The same bridging domain can also carry two separate MAC-VRF instances. Each instance gets its own IP address at the VXLAN tunnel termination point, and a separate underlay path carries each one. Replication between sites can use the same idea, with a Path A and a Path B through controlled overlay-to-underlay paths.

1.7 Hot and Cold Data Across On-Premises Storage and Object Storage​

Think about a dataset your team read every day last month and then did not touch once this month. Its value to the training run did not change, but its place in the storage hierarchy should.

Not all data is accessed the same way, so storage designs usually split it into hot data and cold data. In a hybrid design, hot data stays on on-premises backend servers. Those servers connect to a storage IP fabric or an InfiniBand fabric. Cold data sits off-premises in object storage. You decide which tier a dataset belongs in by how often you read it.

Picture an ML developer who writes the same training script for a local dataset and for one stored in the cloud. The script should not need two versions. The storage layer must make the two locations look alike to the code.

Applications reach both tiers with S3 calls over an HTTPS API. In hybrid deployments, that means TLS over HTTP using the S3 REST API. A framework such as TensorFlow or PyTorch still uses plain POSIX file calls. The parallel file system understands those calls. It moves files between the fast local SSD flash tier, which uses NVMe-o-PCIe, and a scale-optimized tier in the cloud. The ML developer does not need to change the code. For example, a training script keeps the same POSIX calls whichever tier holds the file.

Figure 9-8 shows a hybrid layout. Each AI server has two storage network interface cards. Card A connects to leaf1 and card B connects to leaf2, so a failed leaf does not cut the server off from storage. Both connections carry reads and writes.

On the on-premises side, Fabric A holds leaves 1 and 2 and connects to spines 1 and 2. Fabric B holds spines 3 and 4, with leaves 3 and 4 leading to the storage arrays. Leaves 5 and 6 reach a firewall cluster, and two data center gateways connect on to cloud1 and cloud2 storage. Replication to the cloud keeps snapshots of the local training cluster from being lost. If the building that holds the arrays is damaged, the cloud copy is the one that lets the team restart the job. That is the business reason for the extra cost of the cloud path.

The cloud path is not the same as the local path. Every request to the cloud has a longer trip, a tunnel to build, and a bill attached. Treat the cloud copy as a backup and an archive, not as the place where the training job reads its batches. Access goes through the firewall cluster, so an extra security tunnel is needed. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies names IPsec or HTTPS for that tunnel. Plan for that extra layer before you rely on the cloud copy.

2. NVMe-oF State Machines (NVMe-o-TCP vs NVMe-o-RDMA), Parallel File Systems, and InfiniBand Storage​

2.1 NVMe-oF Architecture: Queues, Capsules, NSID, and PRP vs SGL​

Picture a database server whose local drives are full, while a second team wants the same data on a shared array. The question is whether the shared array can feel as fast as the local drive. NVMe-oF is the answer the industry built, and the parts below explain how it keeps the NVMe behavior while crossing a network.

Local NVMe SSDs are fast because the host talks to them directly over PCIe, using circular Submission Queues and Completion Queues. The catch is that those local queues cannot cross an Ethernet or InfiniBand switch. NVMe over Fabrics keeps the NVMe base specification, the I/O command sets, the management interface, and the queue semantics. Only the transport changes. The PCIe bus gives way to a network binding such as NVMe-o-TCP, NVMe-o-RDMA, or NVMe-o-FC.

NVMe-oF wraps each command and response in a network unit called a capsule. Two kinds of capsule carry most of the traffic. Think of a capsule as a sealed envelope that travels through the network. The command envelope goes out with the request, and the response envelope comes back with the answer. Each envelope carries the same identifier, so the two halves of one operation can be matched later.

  • Command Capsule: Sent from the host initiator to the target controller. It carries a 64-byte Submission Queue Entry plus optional in-capsule write data.
  • Response Capsule: Sent from the target controller back to the host initiator. It carries a 16-byte Completion Queue Entry that reports the status for the matching Command ID.

The two addressing constructs can look similar in a capture, so it helps to picture them as two different labels on one parcel. One label says which room the parcel belongs in. The other label says where the contents sit inside the parcel. Both labels travel with every read and write, and both have to be read correctly.

Every NVMe-oF read (opcode 0x02) or write (opcode 0x01) command carries two addressing constructs. They do different jobs, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion. When you read a capture, check which one you are looking at before you draw a conclusion. A quick look at the opcode and the field names usually settles the question, and it saves you from debugging the wrong layer.

  1. NSID: The Namespace Identifier selects the logical storage volume or partition that the remote NVMe target exposes. One flash array controller can expose several NSIDs, each with its own capacity, block size, or tenant permissions.
  2. PRP1 and PRP2 vs SGL: These descriptors tell the controller where the data lives in host or GPU BAR1 memory. DMA places read data there, or fetches write data from there. PRP1 and PRP2 are two 64-bit Physical Region Page pointers. PRP1 points to the first memory page, and PRP2 points to the second page or to a PRP list. A Scatter-Gather List, or SGL, describes several non-contiguous memory segments inside one 64-byte NVMe command header. SGL is the preferred choice for network fabrics.

2.2 NVMe-o-TCP State Machine, PDUs, and Jumbo-Frame Packing​

Suppose a storage team wants one shared volume across a building, and the network team says the fabric is not tuned for PFC pauses. NVMe-o-TCP is the transport built for that situation. It accepts some loss and delay in exchange for simpler network rules.

Operators choose NVMe-o-TCP when they need shared NVMe block storage over a lossy IP network, or over a link of 40 km or more, without tuning PFC. Each CPU core on the host opens its own TCP connection, and each connection maps 1:1 to a target queue pair. All of these connections use destination TCP port 8009. If you tune a host, count those connections per core. A host with many cores opens many connections, and each connection needs queue depth on the target. A setting that works on a small host can overwhelm the array when the same setting runs on a large one.

NVMe-o-TCP Protocol Data Unit (PDU) Types, Opcodes, and MTU 9000 Packing Math:
- Destination transport port: TCP port 8009
- Capsule / Data PDU Type Codes:
  0x01 = CapsuleCmd (Host-to-controller command capsule carrying 64B SQE: Command ID, opcode, NSID, length, PRP1/PRP2 or SGL)
  0x02 = CapsuleResp (Controller-to-host response capsule carrying 16B CQE)
  0x03 = H2CData (Host-to-controller off-capsule write data PDU)
  0x04 = C2HData (Controller-to-host read data PDU)
- NVMe I/O Opcodes:
  opcode 0x01 = Write (supports in-capsule write with SQE + Data + LAST-PDU flag, or off-capsule write with H2CData 0x03 PDUs)
  opcode 0x02 = Read (CapsuleCmd 0x01 -> sequential C2HData 0x04 PDUs #1..#N -> CapsuleResp 0x02)
- Ordered NVMe-o-TCP PDU Framing Fields:
  Common Header (CH) -> PDU-Specific Header (PSH) -> Header Digest (HDGST) -> Padding (PAD) -> PDU Data (Capsule/Payload) -> Data Digest (DDGST)
- Jumbo-Frame MTU 9000 Multi-PDU Packing:
  Single 4 KiB (4,096B) in-capsule write PDU = 24B (CH+PSH) + 4B (HDGST) + 64B (SQE) + 4,096B (Data) + 4B (DDGST) = 4,192 bytes
  At MTU 9000 (TCP MSS = 8,960 bytes), 2 PDUs = 2 x 4,192B = 8,384 bytes fit inside 1 TCP segment with 576 bytes remaining

The packing math is easier to follow as a worked example. One 4 KiB in-capsule write PDU is 4,192 bytes. That total comes from 24 bytes of common and PDU-specific headers, 4 bytes of header digest, the 64-byte SQE, 4,096 bytes of data, and 4 bytes of data digest. At MTU 9000, the TCP MSS is 8,960 bytes. Two of those PDUs take 8,384 bytes, which leaves 576 bytes of the segment unused. Consider working the arithmetic once yourself, so the packing rules make sense. The lesson is that headers are not free. Each PDU carries a fixed set of bytes before the first byte of data, and a small write pays that overhead as a larger share of its size. Packing two PDUs into one segment is a way to share the cost of the wire between them.

The session then moves through five stages, and each stage must finish before the next one starts. Think of the stages as the steps of a phone call. You dial, the other side answers, you identify yourself, you talk, and then you hang up. If the line drops, the call stops at the step that failed. If your session stops, you can tell which stage you reached.

  1. TCP and optional TLS handshake: The host initiator completes a standard TCP SYN -> SYN-ACK -> ACK handshake to target port 8009. An optional TLS handshake follows when the storage must be encrypted.
  2. ICReq and ICResp PDUs: The host sends an NVMe/TCP ICReq PDU that advertises its NVMe version and the digest flags HDGST and DDGST. It also sends the PDU data alignment and host identifier fields. The controller answers with an ICResp PDU.
  3. NVMe/TCP Connect request and response: The host binds the TCP connection to a specific Controller ID. It then negotiates the Admin or I/O queue IDs (QID) and the queue depth.
  4. Data transfer and jumbo-frame packing: The session exchanges CapsuleCmd, H2CData, C2HData, and CapsuleResp PDUs. On a storage IP fabric with jumbo frames at MTU 9000, one TCP segment can carry two or more NVMe-o-TCP PDUs back to back.
  5. Session termination: Either endpoint sends H2CTermReq or C2HTermReq, followed by a final CapsuleResp CQE status. This releases the NSID, the Command IDs, and the queue bindings before the TCP socket closes.

If you plan to rely on the packing, verify MTU 9000 on every hop of the storage path first. A single hop that still uses the default frame size breaks the packing for every session that crosses it, and the failure looks like a slow array rather than a configuration error.

2.3 NVMe-o-RDMA (RoCEv2) State Machine: TCP 8009 -> UDP 4420 -> UDP 4791​

Training clusters can reach a point where TCP processing burns too much CPU and adds tail latency. NVMe-o-RDMA moves the storage transport into the NIC's RDMA engine instead. It delivers up to 66% lower write latency, and it lets GPUDirect Storage DMA straight into GPU HBM. Over a lossless RoCEv2 fabric, the session passes through three ports and two protocols. If a firewall blocks any one of those three ports, session bring-up stalls partway through. If you see a stall, check the three ports before you blame the storage array. A stall at discovery points to TCP 8009, a stall during the RDMA handshake points to UDP 4420, and a stall during data transfer points to UDP 4791.

Read the table phase by phase. Each phase depends on the one before it, so a blocked port at any step stops the session at that step. Think of the phases as the checkpoints on a route, where a closed gate at one checkpoint stops everyone behind it. Each phase depends on the one before it, so a blocked port at any step stops the session at that step. You can use the phases as a checklist while you debug the session.

2.4 High-Performance Parallel File Systems and GPUDirect Storage Integration​

Picture ten training nodes that all open the same dataset folder at the same moment. A block volume alone cannot tell them which files belong to whom, because each host sees only its own session. That coordination is the job of a file layer on top.

Raw NVMe-oF block volumes give each host a 1:1 session to a target namespace. They do not coordinate file metadata across many GPU clients at once. A shared POSIX namespace at high speed therefore needs a parallel file system layered on top of NVMe-o-RDMA. Common choices include WekaFS, VAST, Lustre, GPFS, and BeeGFS, running over RoCEv2 or InfiniBand.

Parallel File System Striping and GPUDirect Storage (GDS) Parameters:
- Parallel file systems: WekaFS, VAST, Lustre, GPFS (IBM Storage Scale), BeeGFS
- WekaFS striping: distributes file data and metadata across all on-premises NVMe storage servers (Servers 1..8)
- Standard AI file extensions & POSIX syscalls: .pt, .npy, .ckpt, .h5, .bin via open(), read(), write(), stat(), unlink()
- Two-tier storage hierarchy: Tier 1 hot flash over RDMA (RoCEv2 / InfiniBand) + Tier 2 cold snapshots over HTTPS/TLS S3 REST APIs; VAST runs multipath NFS over RDMA
- GPUDirect Storage (GDS) zero-copy pipeline (PyTorch v2.7.0+):
  cudaMalloc -> cuFileBufRegister -> PIN to direct IO -> PCIe DMA directly between external RoCEv2/IB arrays and GPU 1..8 HBM via NIC A and NIC B (100G/200G or 400G/800G) bypassing host CPU and system DRAM

Two parts of this stack are worth understanding before you tune anything. Both of them change what the file system does with a single read, so a change to either one can move the performance in ways you do not expect. The first is how the file system spreads data out. For example, a shared training dataset is a case for the file layer.

  • Parallel data and metadata striping: Rather than funnel every client through one NFS gateway, WekaFS spreads both file data and metadata in small chunks across all on-premises NVMe storage servers. When PyTorch or TensorFlow reads checkpoint files through POSIX syscalls, the local client turns each call into parallel RDMA requests against Tier 1 hot flash. Cold snapshots move to Tier 2 cloud object storage over HTTPS S3 REST APIs.
  • GPUDirect Storage zero-copy DMA: When PyTorch or the CUDA runtime allocates GPU memory with cudaMalloc and registers it with cuFileBufRegister, the GPU memory pages are pinned for direct I/O. The GDS library then programs the storage NIC driver directly over PCIe. Blocks move between the external arrays and GPU HBM through the dedicated storage NICs, without bouncing through host CPU cores or system DRAM.

Use a parallel file system when many GPU clients need the same files. Use raw NVMe-oF volumes when each session needs its own namespace. Think of a shared file system as a library with one catalog that everyone uses, and a raw volume as a private locker that only one person opens.

2.5 InfiniBand for Storage: 16-Bit LID (48,000 Nodes), Frame Format, and 64B CBFC Credits​

Suppose a storage team plans a native InfiniBand array next to a RoCEv2 array, and someone asks why the frames look so different on the wire. The answer is that the InfiniBand frame drops the Ethernet envelope and keeps the same storage conversation inside it.

When native InfiniBand carries NVMe-o-RDMA and GPUDirect Storage, the RDMA handshake and the NVMe capsule payload look the same as they do on RoCEv2. The difference is the frame. The outer Ethernet, IP, and UDP headers give way to the native InfiniBand frame. That removes the L3 and L4 header overhead, and it cuts switch latency to roughly 300 ns.

Native InfiniBand Storage Frame Header Pipeline:
LRH (S-LID, D-LID, VL) -> GRH (Optional Inter-Subnet) -> BTH (Opcode, Dest QP) -> ETH (Src QP / RDMA) -> NVMe-o-RDMA Capsule + Data -> ICRC + VCRC
Native InfiniBand Storage Addressing, Integrity, and Flow Control Specs:
- Local Routing Header (LRH) addressing: 16-bit Local Identifier (LID, 65,536 total values) assigned by the Subnet Manager (SM) via per-switch Subnet Manager Agents (SMAs)
- Single-subnet node limit: up to 48,000 end nodes per InfiniBand subnet (e.g., 32,000 nodes fit in one subnet; 60,000 nodes require inter-subnet routing via the Global Routing Header GRH)
- Frame CRCs: Invariant CRC (ICRC, end-to-end immutable fields) + Variant CRC (VCRC, hop-by-hop link fields)
- Virtual Lanes (VLs) and Credit-Based Flow Control (CBFC): up to 16 VLs per physical link (typically 8-12 active data VLs); 1 CBFC credit in the Link Layer Header (LLH) = 64 bytes of buffer space (64 credits required for a 4,096-byte storage block); 10 km optical spec reach

The numbers in that block make two checks easy. You can verify the subnet size against the node count on a whiteboard, and you can verify the credit count on the back of an envelope. Those quick checks catch design mistakes before anyone orders hardware. A 32,000-node cluster fits inside one subnet. A 60,000-node cluster is above the 48,000-node limit, so it needs inter-subnet routing through the GRH. For the flow control side, one credit covers 64 bytes of buffer space. A 4,096-byte block therefore needs 4,096 divided by 64, or 64 credits. You can repeat that division for any block size you plan to store.

2.6 GPUDirect Storage: Logical View and CUDA API Orchestration​

Picture a checkpoint that must leave the GPUs, cross the server, and land on an array. Each extra copy on that trip costs time, and it also taxes the host CPU that has to run the copy. GPUDirect Storage is the feature that removes those copies from the path.

GPUDirect Storage lets a GPU place data straight onto storage arrays. The usual path makes the CPU copy every byte. GDS skips that copy. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies describes the result as less jitter, much better bus bandwidth, and much lower CPU load. It also means fewer competing connections on each server. For you as an operator, that means a quieter host.

Figure 9-19 gives the logical view. The top section shows a server chassis labeled PCIe. Inside it, a central processing unit and system memory connect over the PCIe bus. Below them sit eight graphics processing units, GPU1 through GPU8, so the chassis holds a CPU, system memory, and eight GPUs. Each GPU has its own NIC, and each NIC connects to its own leaf switch. Eight spine switches form a full mesh with those leaves, and each leaf links to every spine in both directions. Two more dedicated NICs, NICA and NICB, connect the server to a ROCEv2 Storage cloud and to external storage arrays. Their arrows show two-way traffic, while each GPU NIC sends over a unidirectional link to its leaf. The GPUs place their outputs over the RoCEv2 NICs without interrupting the CPU core. Trace the figure from the GPU NICs to the leaves, and you will see every hop.

That design relaxes the demand on system memory. Think of system memory as a shared kitchen counter. If every ingredient must be set down there first, the cooks wait for counter space. With direct placement, the ingredients go straight to the cook's station, and the counter stays clear for other work. After the direct placement, the GPU can start its next task. The server does not need a large multi-core CPU or costly local memory to do it. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies notes that this matters little in a small cluster. At 100,000 GPUs or more, the saving can have a large cost impact.

GDS is also built into the CUDA API. A developer who calls the API does not need to write the NIC driver code, because the library handles that layer. The developer's job is to register the buffer and to keep the calls in the right order. CUDA is NVIDIA's parallel computing platform and programming interface. The CUDA layer sets up the NIC driver for the RoCEv2 or InfiniBand transfer. In practice, that means the framework asks for a registered buffer once, and the transfers then reuse it without touching the CPU for every byte. It does that without using a CPU core or any other system memory. When you read a GDS trace, expect the CPU to stay out of the data path. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies adds that PyTorch v2.7.0 and later also support GDS API calls directly. It also says that RDMA access over InfiniBand or RoCEv2 is the one storage form where a GPU places data directly onto a remote SSD.

Confirm GDS registration on a real node, using the check in the commands section, before you plan a design around it. A design built on a feature that the node does not register will look fine on paper and then fall back to the slower path in production.

3. Ultra Ethernet Consortium (UEC Specification v1.0): Architecture, PDS/SES, Encapsulations, and Delivery Modes​

Picture an Ethernet network team whose AI cluster grows from a few racks to a whole building. The team already knows its switches, but it now needs the fabric to behave like a supercomputer interconnect. The specification discussed here is the industry's answer to that need.

The Ultra Ethernet Specification v1.0 is a major step for Ethernet. The Ultra Ethernet Consortium (UEC) released it on June 11, 2025, under the Linux Foundation. It was built for HPC, AI, cloud infrastructure, and data-intensive enterprise environments. Its goal is simple to state: make Ethernet fit the demands of modern GPU clusters. The hard part is the list of details that must all change together, and the rest of this chapter walks through that list one part at a time.

Ethernet and IP fabrics have already proven themselves in AI data centers. Operators know how to run them, how to monitor them, and how to buy them from many vendors. The open question is how to make them behave like a dedicated interconnect for training. Production deployments have reached 100,000-GPU server clusters, and thousands of 400 Gbps and 800 Gbps ports connect those servers together.

InfiniBand used to be the usual choice for this kind of cluster. Ethernet has since caught up on speed, and it brings a wider set of vendors, which is why buyers now compare the two on cost and on openness as well as on raw performance. Once Ethernet was tuned for lossless operation, 800 Gbps Ethernet became a more cost-competitive option. It offered the same performance, better scalability, and an open standard. Many vendors support both the switch side and the NIC side.

Fine-tuned Ethernet with RoCEv2 and DCQCN still had drawbacks. Think of the tuning as a set of dials that must be turned again each time the traffic pattern changes. A dial set for one workload can misbehave for the next one. The biggest one is that operators had to retune it as AI workloads changed. The way server NICs announce end-to-end reordering can change. So can the handling of reordered packets, or the number of sessions that run in parallel based on buffer size.

The network needs to understand what end hosts can do, and it needs to adapt to them. It can set buffers or pick a load-balancing method for one workload. Think of the network as a referee that adjusts the rules to each game. The rules should stay fixed for the whole match, because a referee who changes them in the middle of a play confuses both teams. Then it should keep that choice steady for the whole job. If you change that choice mid-job, you break the steady state the job depends on.

AI models keep growing in parameters and tokens. Each jump in model size adds more gradient traffic, and the fabric has to keep up with that growth without a redesign every year. That pressure is what pushes the standard toward very large fabrics. Data center networks will need ports of 400 Gbps or more to synchronize GPU servers. For a training domain, the IP fabric should be ready for more than a million GPU nodes. Reaching that scale takes more optimization, such as faster ramp time, delivery mode detection, and both link-level and end-to-end congestion control. UEC wants to standardize these at the industry level.

Standards cannot stop at the switch. A switch that offers a feature is only useful when the NIC on each server can use it too. That is why the specification covers both ends of the link, and why the chapter keeps returning to the NIC. End-host NIC drivers need them too, which makes this a full-stack solution. The switch understands what the end host needs, and the end host understands real-time network conditions. For example, a UEC NIC can react to congestion by changing its source UDP port. When you size a fabric, you need the switch and the NIC to agree on that behavior.

The UEC decided to define a new transport, link, and software stack. A new stack is a bigger step than a patch, but it avoids carrying old assumptions forward. Think of it as a new building designed for today's traffic, rather than an extension on an old house. The aim is more performance and a fabric that is easier to deploy in a plug-and-play way. The table below lists the main motivations for that redesign. It puts a strong focus on end-host NIC optimization.

  • Scalability: Modern AI training clusters and supercomputers can have tens of thousands of nodes. Legacy protocols often struggle to keep performance at that size.
  • Reliability and congestion control: Congestion must be managed well when several high-bandwidth applications run at the same time.

3.1 UEC Working Groups, Service Profiles (AI Base, AI Full, HPC), and Core Terminology​

As Ethernet AI clusters scale toward a million endpoints, legacy RoCEv2 hits three walls. Static ECMP flow hashing creates tail-latency collisions. A single dropped packet triggers a Go-Back-N retransmission of the entire window. Hop-by-hop PFC pause frames also risk spreading congestion across unrelated jobs. UEC Specification v1.0 answers these walls. Released on June 11, 2025 by more than 70 member companies under the Linux Foundation, it replaces the InfiniBand Base Transport Header with a multipath-native Ethernet transport stack. Think of the old header as a label that assumes one fixed road for each conversation. The new stack is built for many roads at once, so the sender can choose a different path for each packet.

UEC Specification v1.0 Structure and Three Service Profiles:
- Eight Working Groups:
  1. Physical Layer (FEC statistics, link fault signaling, modified 64B/66B PCS encoding with out-of-band Control Ordered Sets CtlOS)
  2. Link Layer (Link Layer Reliability LLR, Packet Rate Improvement PRI, Credit-Based Flow Control CBFC)
  3. Transport Layer (Ultra Ethernet Transport UET, PDS, SES, TSS, NSCC, RCCC, DSCP packet trimming)
  4. Software (Libfabric APIs fi_mr and fi_collective, In-Network Collectives INC)
  5. Storage, 6. Management, 7. Performance & Debug, 8. Compliance
- Three Negotiated Service Profiles:
  - AI Base Profile: mandatory ROD and RUD delivery modes + mandatory NSCC congestion control over lossy or lossless 400G/800G Ethernet
  - AI Full Profile: adds RUDI (idempotent unordered writes for gradient aggregation) as mandatory; UUD optional
  - HPC Profile: strictest latency and feature rules; supports ROD, RUD, RUDI, and optional UUD
- Seven Core UET Constructs:
  1. Fabric Endpoint (FEP; sFEP for INC-capable switches)
  2. Fabric Address (FA; IPv4 or IPv6 via SLAAC)
  3. Packet Delivery Context (PDC; SPDCID, DPDCID, bound to 1 Congestion Control Context CCC_ID)
  4. Packet Delivery Sublayer (PDS; SYN, PSNs, Clear PSN Offset, ACK/NACK/SACK, req_cc_state, ACK_CC)
  5. Semantic Sublayer (SES; Libfabric fi_mr RKEY validation & fi_collective, Opcode RMA WRITE 0x01, TAGGED_SEND 0x80, DEFERRABLE SEND, Message ID, Buffer Offset, Match Bits, Request Length)
  6. JobID, PIDonFEP, and Resource Index (RI)
  7. Transport Security Sublayer (TSS; AES-GCM encryption header + trailing Integrity Check Value ICV)

Reading a UEC packet capture or configuring a UET NIC requires seven core architectural constructs. Each one has a clear job, so learn them in order.

  1. Fabric Endpoint: Any server NIC or switch port that takes part in the UEC fabric. Switch endpoints that can run In-Network Collectives are called sFEPs.
  2. Fabric Address: The IPv4 or IPv6 address that identifies an FEP. Plug-and-play provisioning through IPv6 SLAAC often assigns it automatically.
  3. Packet Delivery Context: A logical point-to-point transport channel between an initiator FEP and a target FEP. It is identified by SPDCID and DPDCID, and it is bound to one Congestion Control Context (CCC_ID).
  4. Packet Delivery Sublayer: The lower UET sublayer. It handles PDC session setup, Packet Sequence Numbers, Clear PSN Offset, ACK/NACK/SACK processing, and congestion control state.
  5. Semantic Sublayer: The upper UET sublayer. It translates Libfabric calls (fi_mr and fi_collective) into wire semantics, and it encodes opcodes, message IDs, buffer offsets, match bits, and request lengths.
  6. JobID, PIDonFEP, and Resource Index: These three fields replace the legacy RoCEv2 BTH Queue Pair number. JobID identifies the distributed job across the cluster. PIDonFEP identifies the target OS process context, and Resource Index identifies the completion or receive queue.
  7. Transport Security Sublayer: A built-in UET encryption and authentication header that uses AES-GCM with a trailing Integrity Check Value. It provides end-to-end confidentiality, integrity, and anti-replay protection without external MACsec overhead.

More than 70 vendors and institutions formed working groups, and each one focused on a single area of improvement. Think of the groups as the trades on a large construction site, with electricians, plumbers, and framers each working on their own part of one building. Their work was merged into one document for the first specification, released in June 2025 as a standard reference. You do not need every group's role to follow the technologies. Knowing which groups exist still helps, because it shows which features may appear in a later version. This chapter focuses on the Transport and Link Layer working groups and how they connect to the Software working group. Start with those two groups, and the rest of the chapter will read more easily.

The Link Layer working group looks at the connection between two end nodes. Think of a link as a single stretch of road between two intersections. Problems that start on that stretch should be fixed there, before they spread to the rest of the route. That can be two UEC switches, or a UEC switch and a server. The group studies how to improve congestion control and reliable transport on each link. The goal is faster job completion and less tail latency. Three functions stand out, and you will meet each one again later in this chapter.

  • Link Layer Reliability (LLR): LLR reduces the latency cost of packet loss. Recovery happens on the link, so the end host does not have to handle every retry.
  • Packet Rate Improvement (PRI): PRI aims to raise the frame rate. It cuts per-frame overhead and keeps only the header fields that AI transmissions need.
  • Credit-Based Flow Control (CBFC): CBFC aims to replace the RoCEv2 PFC with a more efficient credit system on each link. InfiniBand already uses credit-based flow control for storage transport, and UEC applies the same credit idea to Ethernet.

The Transport working group develops Ultra Ethernet Transport (UET), which is one of the most important pieces for end-to-end integration. Think of UET as the part of the stack that carries the message from one end of the route to the other, while the link groups fix problems on each stretch along the way. UET uses a new data packet format. Instead of carrying an InfiniBand BTH inside a RoCEv2 UDP frame, it defines a new set of headers after the UDP header. The group also proposes packet trimming, Receiver Credit Congestion Control (RCCC), and Network Signal Congestion Control (NSCC). NSCC and RCCC both influence the entropy used for multipathing.

UEC uses two levels of congestion control and signaling. One level runs end to end between servers. The other runs on the link, and some of its functions are optional. UET also brings authentication, encryption, and a UET security header into the packet itself. That replaces the separate MACsec functions used with encrypted RoCEv2.

The Semantic Sublayer (SES) keeps the two endpoints in sync. It lets the software layer, Libfabric, track the state of each transaction. The Transport group also defines the basic packet delivery modes. Those modes set how two end hosts agree to communicate and whether packets must arrive in order.

The Software working group defines In-Network Collectives (INC). It also defines how collective communication libraries (CCLs) use fabric resources efficiently. The group proposes an INC manager and an INC switch agent as new AI data center components. InfiniBand deployments already use similar components, and UEC took inspiration from them.

UET borrows the strongest ideas from both worlds. Think of it as a hybrid car that takes the best of two engines. The credit idea comes from one design, and the open, distributed addressing comes from the other. From InfiniBand it takes credit mechanisms, CCL and INC support, and plug-and-play behavior. From Ethernet it takes a distributed model, where entries do not depend on a central state. IPv6 is a good example of that approach at massive scale. Plug-and-play address assignment with SLAAC, native to IPv6, is planned for UET fabrics too. You can think of UET as a design that takes the best parts of each parent.

The API is a major focus for the Software group. An API that lets the application state its needs is useful, because the network can then choose the right service instead of guessing from the packet headers. It is looking at Libfabric, an open-source API that lets applications state their needs for message and packet ordering. UET then picks the transport service that matches those needs. The group also defines the AI Base, AI Full, and HPC profiles.

Other groups matter as well. Certification and measurement work decide whether a product from one vendor works with a product from another, and that work often takes as long as the design itself. The Compliance working group defines the tests that show a device meets UEC standards. Companies such as Spirent and Keysight help define how performance is measured and how KPIs are checked. The Management working group makes the new UET standard deployable in production networks.

The Physical Layer working group is the most disruptive. It changes the lowest levels of the stack, so existing Ethernet gear may not be fully compatible. That work will likely take the longest to reach scale-out products. The Storage working group stays somewhat separate, and it becomes relevant once the Transport and Link Layer work reaches switches and NICs.

Here is what each working group covers in the first specification. Read the list as a map of who owns which part of the stack, so you know whom to ask when a feature behaves unexpectedly.

  • Physical Layer working group: Creates specifications that improve the performance, latency, and management of the Ethernet physical layer, including the medium and the clients on that layer.
  • Link Layer working group: Improves the performance, latency, and management of the Ethernet link layer.
  • Transport Layer working group: Creates an AI and HPC transport with better throughput, latency, scalability, and management over Ethernet.
  • Software working group: Creates specifications and an open-source software API for AI and HPC use cases.
  • Storage working group: Works on compatibility with standard physical connections, link-level features, and packet encoding. It also covers transport services, security, and RDMA API compatibility with the Software group.
  • Compliance working group: Makes sure devices and services follow UEC standards, and defines the tests that validate them.
  • Management working group: Focuses on fabric manageability, including topology discovery, monitoring, and interoperability across vendors.
  • Performance and Debug working group: Defines performance benchmarks and adds debugging capabilities.

3.2 PDC Session Handshake, UDP-Based Encapsulation for UET (UDP Port 49150), and UET over IP (IP Protocol 253)​

Picture two servers that want to talk for the first time and must agree on the rules before they send anything. Each side needs to know which delivery mode, which profile and which congestion method the other side supports. The handshake below is how they settle those questions.

Before data moves on a Packet Delivery Context, initiator FEP A and target FEP B run a lightweight three-step PDS handshake. The handshake avoids slow out-of-band queue-pair exchanges. When Reliable Ordered Delivery is selected, every message that shares {JobID, source PIDonFEP, destination PIDonFEP, RI, Traffic Class} is pinned to a single PDC. That pinning is why you should set the delivery mode before the first message leaves the host.

PDC Session Establishment Handshake Sequence:
1. Initiator FEP A -> Target FEP B: sends PDS packet with pds.flags.syn = 1, initial PSN = 101, Source PDC ID (SPDCID = 201), Clear PSN Offset, and requested delivery mode (ROD_REQ 0x03 or RUD_REQ)
2. Target FEP B -> Initiator FEP A: allocates local PDC (301) and replies with ACK = 101, SPDCID = 301, DPDCID = 201
3. Initiator FEP A -> Target FEP B: records DPDCID = 301, clears SYN bit (pds.flags.syn = 0), and transmits subsequent data packets (PSN = 102, SPDCID = 201, DPDCID = 301)

Two servers form a one-to-one relationship during session setup. Think of it as two people who agree to a private phone line before they start talking. Once the line is open, both sides know whom they are speaking to and which rules apply to the call. The initiator and target start that relationship at the API and Libfabric levels. Libfabric turns the requirements into network and target memory parameters, and SES and PDS then use those parameters. Each server's stack runs from the application down through Libfabric, SES, PDC, PDS, UDP, IP, and MAC.

Session setup begins with endpoint discovery. Each side must first learn that the other side exists and can be reached. Only after that does the conversation about profiles, reordering, and congestion method begin. Each FEP gets a fabric address, which is typically IPv4 or IPv6. Each FEP also gets relative addressing based on JobID and PIDonFEP for the PDC. The next phase exchanges supported profiles, such as AI Base or AI Full, along with transport capabilities and reordering support. The congestion management method for the PDC is negotiated in that same phase. When you debug a stuck session, check which of these phases never completed.

A real capture makes the PDS fields concrete. Reading a capture is the fastest way to tell whether your switches and your NICs agree, because the flags and identifiers show what each side actually sent. A sample PDS packet was captured and decoded with the Spirent implementation of the UEC emulator, then opened in Wireshark. In this capture, SYN is set in the PDS, and the ROD request is advertised. The delivery mode was set indirectly, through the Libfabric settings at the software level.

The frame reads from top to bottom. It starts with Ethernet II, then IPv4, then the UET transport. Inside the PDS, the Flags field shows CRC, RETX, ACK Request, and SYN set. The PSN is 13, and both PDC IDs are 0. The ACK arrives in frame 3253, and the payload closes with a CRC of fcfdfeff.

The SES part of a second capture decodes the JobID, PIDonFEP, and Resource Index. That request is a TAGGED_SEND, an operation that uses match bits to choose a buffer. The Job ID is 1, and the index is 15. The match bits are 0x0000000000000001, and the request length is 4096 bytes.

A UDP-based capture shows the same ideas. Its PDS is a RUD_REQ with flags 0x48 and PSN 2607. Both source and destination PDC IDs are 2. Its SES is a DEFERRABLE SEND with Message ID 23. The PDC source and destination and the delivery mode stay the same for the whole transfer. Only the PSNs and ACKs change.

The Message ID stays constant across a set of writes or sends, which helps track, match, and complete each operation. Think of the Message ID as a tracking number on a parcel. The sender and receiver use it to match the ACK to the right write, even when many writes are in flight at once. An ACK for an RUD request is shorter, and it refers only to the Message ID and Job ID.

The IP-only capture shows the lighter format. Its PDS carries an Entropy value, and there is no UDP layer. The flags are 0x48 with CRC set, and SYN is not set. The SES is a WRITE RMA opcode 0x01 with Message ID 1, Job ID 1, and Index 15. Even without UDP, Job ID, PIDonFEP, and Resource Index still map to the GPU memory region that Libfabric defines.

UEC Specification v1.0 defines two wire encapsulations for UET packets. Both endpoints must be configured identically before the PDC is created.

UDP-based encapsulation can also add security. A Transport Security Sublayer (TSS) header sits right after UDP, and the AES-GCM ICV is placed after the UET payload. Together they give end-to-end confidentiality, integrity, and anti-replay protection for AI workloads. A UET CRC is optional and can be used instead of TSS, but never both at once. If you enable TSS, you turn the UET CRC off.

Encryption changes how switches can balance traffic. A switch that balances on hidden fields has to fall back on the fields it can still see. That is a trade-off between privacy and load balance, and the team should choose it on purpose. Once TSS hides the SES fields, switches cannot read the Resource Index or JobID, so packet spraying or UDP source port load balancing must be used instead. The SES opcode says what kind of write or send is happening. In RoCEv2 that information sat in the BTH. Here, the Resource Index, JobID, PIDonFEP, and buffer offset tell the target where the data goes in memory. Consider this when you plan the switch hashing for an encrypted job.

CRC means cyclic redundancy checking at the UET layer. A CRC is a short checksum that the receiver recomputes, and a mismatch tells it that the bits changed on the way. Think of it as a seal that breaks when someone tampers with the envelope. It protects data integrity across the transport path, and NICs can use it. It helps most on lossy transport, where corruption can slip past hop-by-hop Ethernet checks. Whether it applies depends on the packet delivery mode of the PDC. For ROD, it can still check end-to-end integrity when packets arrive in order.

IP-only encapsulation also allows a CRC or TSS, but the specification says not to use both. TSS sits immediately after the Entropy field in this format. Like UDP-based encapsulation, the PDS carries PSNs and ACK or NACK messages. If NSCC is used, the NACK carries entropy so the sender can adapt it. In this format the sender changes the Entropy field instead of the UDP source port. You will see that field in the capture, so know where to look.

3.3 The Four UEC Packet Delivery Modes (ROD, RUD, RUDI, UUD)​

Suppose a training job sprays its packets across every spine switch to balance the load, and the receiving NIC starts to see gaps in the sequence. That is the everyday tension between load balancing and reliable delivery, and the delivery modes below are how UEC handles it.

In RoCEv2, spraying packets across multiple spine switches causes out-of-order arrivals at the target NIC. RoCEv2 reads those out-of-order arrivals as packet loss, and it flushes the entire 64-packet window. Through Libfabric, UET lets an application pick one of four delivery modes per PDC. It uses selective retransmission, so only the single missing PSN is resent.

Selective Retransmission vs RoCEv2 Go-Back-N Penalty:
- In-flight window size: 64 packets (PSN 1..64)
- Single packet loss at packet #1:
  RoCEv2 Go-Back-N flushes and retransmits all 64 packets
  UET (RUD / RUDI + packet trimming) selectively retransmits only 1 packet (saving 63 packets, a 64.0x retransmission reduction)

Put the penalty in plain numbers. The point is not that the retry is slow in one step. The point is that go-back-N repeats far more work than the loss itself requires, and the extra work crowds the link at the moment it is busiest. With a 64-packet window and packet 1 lost, Go-Back-N resends all 64 packets. Selective retry resends one. That is 63 packets that never have to cross the fabric twice.

Why does the delivery mode matter for AI? Some traffic must arrive in order, such as a control message that sets up a job. Other traffic, such as gradient writes that add into the same memory, can arrive in any order and still produce the right answer. Matching the mode to the traffic keeps the fabric fast without breaking the job. RoCEv2 has many limits on which traffic can run in order and which can run unordered. The usable mode often depends on the NIC vendor and the operation type. Writing into the same memory space differs from writing into different spaces. Writing once also differs from writing the same sector many times. UEC lets an AI developer set these needs through Libfabric API calls. You set them once, and the stack carries them down to the wire.

PyTorch has an interface to Open MPI, and Open MPI connects to Libfabric. Some settings can be made directly through the API. Libfabric turns them into PDS and SES settings, and UET carries them across the fabric. The packet delivery mode settings follow the same path, from the AI application down to the transport. If you configure a job, you will touch that path at the top, not the bottom.

MPI is a widely adopted API standard for parallel computing. It began in HPC, and it now plays a key role as a high-level interface for UEC. MPI connects to Libfabric and to AI frameworks such as PyTorch. Open MPI, when connected to Libfabric, also gives an interface to NCCL or RCCL for collective communication. Open MPI itself handles process coordination and CPU-side messaging.

The NCCL or RCCL connection is not needed when native MPI collectives or Libfabric-managed collectives are used. MPICH, an MPI alternative to Open MPI, supports MPI_Allreduce and MPI_Alltoall as documented collective types. In most cases, reliable delivery is the right choice. Its ACK step costs some speed, but it confirms every packet. A NACK can be used when lower PSNs are still missing and higher PSNs arrived fine.

RUD, ROD, and RUDI are all reliable modes, and only the ordering choice changes between them. UUD is the unreliable and unordered option. Think of it as a postcard. It goes out quickly and costs little, but nothing confirms that it arrived, so it suits telemetry better than a gradient. Each profile has a delivery mode for each operation type, such as read, write, or send. One passage in the source text says all profiles support RUD and ROD, and that only HPC supports RUDI. The profile table, however, lists RUDI as mandatory in AI Full. Check the exact profile rules in the UEC specification before you rely on either statement.

3.4 Protocol Stack, Network Architectures, and Existing Ethernet​

Suppose your Ethernet vendor tells you that a new transport needs a new physical layer. The first question is whether your existing cables and switch ports still work. The answer below is that UEC keeps standard Ethernet at the bottom.

The biggest early worry was that Ethernet and the physical layer would stop being compatible with IEEE standards. That would slow adoption of UEC and its transport and congestion features. UEC avoids the problem. It keeps standard Ethernet and changes only the layers after UDP, along with the end-to-end semantics. The first focus is the new UET packet format. You should look at that header first when you read a capture.

From top to bottom, the stack holds applications, software APIs such as CCL, MPI, and OpenSHMEM, and the transport layer over UDP and IP. Below that sit a security layer, the IP layer, the Ethernet link layer, and the Ethernet PHY. The transport layer includes SES message semantics, PDS packet delivery with RUD and ROD options, and congestion management. The link layer adds LLDP negotiation, packet rate improvements, LLR, and CBFC. LLDP lets the two ends of a link discover what each one supports before they agree on the rules, which avoids a mismatch that would otherwise show up only as a slow link.

The UEC Ethernet physical layer is a longer-term option. Its main jobs are FEC statistics for predicting link quality and link fault signaling. It also uses modified 64B/66B PCS encoding to run out-of-band signaling inside the physical layer. The optional control ordered set message, CtlOS, supports CBFC and LLR on each segment.

UET does not require a new network design. Most teams can keep the layout they already run and change the transport settings on top of it, which lowers the cost of trying the new features. The IP Clos three-stage and five-stage designs keep their place, even for UEC scale-out clusters. The right design depends on the target scale and the expected performance. For about 100,000 or 1 million GPUs, a five-stage or seven-stage architecture is needed, along with load-balancing mechanisms on the Ethernet and IP switches. If your fabric is already a three-stage Clos, you do not need to start over.

The lossless fabric stays optional for UEC. Think of the lossless link as a safety rail. It helps on a narrow road, but a well-designed end-to-end system can drive safely without it, which is what the specification aims for. NIC-level reliable delivery and end-to-end congestion control are meant to deliver good performance even when not every segment is lossless. That is why the first specification keeps link-layer optimization optional. Those optimizations can still raise scale and performance a lot.

For latency-sensitive inference, the five-stage topology may not be used. Every extra stage adds a hop, and an inference reply that waits for several hops feels slow to the user. A shorter path helps that workload even when the training fabric is larger. A collapsed spine design or a three-stage IP fabric takes its place. Some UEC networks add INC and CCL features on leaf-spine topologies to save bandwidth and latency. That needs stronger spine devices and better management platforms, and the fabric may also need a controller such as an INC manager.

Most UEC IP fabrics keep a high port radix from leaf to spine. Those switches must balance traffic based on the semantics negotiated at the SES layer. Early on, some feared the Ethernet header would change to reduce segment latency. Compression or field removal remains an option, but it is optional, like the link-layer features.

Existing 400 Gbps and 800 Gbps switches with enough Ethernet and IP ports can therefore carry UEC, at least for the AI Base profile. UEC-ready switches are mostly Ethernet switches that parse deep enough to read some UEC fields for load balancing. With JobID or Resource Index in the packet, the source UDP port must join the load-balancing hash. RoCEv2's QPair-based balancing no longer works, because the BTH is gone. So when you move a RoCEv2 fabric to UET, you must change the hash configuration too. Think of the hash as the rule that decides which lane each car takes. If the rule still looks for a label that no longer exists, every car lands in the same lane.

Most networks will keep leaf-spine-super spine designs with more 800 Gbps, 1.6 Tbps, and 3.2 Tbps ports. AI Data Center Network Design and Technologies expects architectures to move toward DragonFly, DragonFly+, and Torus. It also expects a shift toward scale-up systems rather than scale-out. The change is mostly in switch software features, not in the design itself. A team that already runs a leaf-spine fabric can often upgrade the software and keep the cables and the rack layout.

The first UEC networks will likely share the fabric with existing RoCEv2 servers. Think of the shared fabric as a road with two lanes of different traffic, one for trucks that must never stop and one for cars that can slow down. The queues that separate the two lanes are the planning work the rest of this paragraph describes. The same ToR may serve UEC-ready servers and RoCEv2 GPU servers. RoCEv2 servers keep the lossless approach. UEC-ready servers can use best-effort delivery and rely on end-to-end control through RCCC or NSCC. You can run both kinds of server on one ToR, but you must plan the queues.

Lossless mechanisms such as PFC-DSCP, LLR, and CBFC need careful planning, and one of the link-level options must be chosen for a shared link. For a native UEC fabric, link-layer control, reliable transport, and end-to-end control work well together. When two workload types must share a fabric, the lossless queue still uses RoCEv2, and the rest of the congestion control happens at the server NIC. Keep a written list of which queue each workload uses, because a silent change to that list is a common cause of mixed-traffic stalls.

Path isolation can also help. Think of a separate overlay as a second road built beside the first one. New UEC work can use the new road, and the existing jobs keep using the old one without any change. The multi-tenancy options from the earlier IP routing discussion can carry new UEC workloads as a separate overlay, which keeps them isolated from existing AI and ML workloads. That keeps them away from the existing jobs, so you can add them without moving those jobs.

4. UEC Congestion Management (NSCC, RCCC, CBFC), Packet Trimming, LLR, and In-Network Collectives (INC)​

Flexible delivery modes still need congestion management. Picture a highway where every car can choose its own lane. Without a speed sign and a way to slow the flow at the on-ramps, the lanes still jam. Congestion control is that speed sign, and it works on both ends of the trip. You want the initiator and the target to react to congestion quickly, because that cuts retransmissions. A slow reaction lets the queue grow, and a growing queue turns a small burst into a long stall for every job that shares the link. The response must not slow an AI workload for longer than necessary, and it must not slow workloads that caused none of the congestion. RoCEv2 AI workloads suffered on this point, because PFC backpressure also hit servers that were not part of the congestion. You can see why that matters for any shared rack.

UEC has two main types of congestion management. Think of them as a city's traffic lights and its road signs. One part tells the drivers at each intersection what to do, and the other part keeps each segment of the road from filling up. The first is end-to-end, using either NSCC, which is managed at the originator, or RCCC, which is managed at the target. The second is segment or link-level control, which uses CBFC. Each PDC gets a congestion control context, CCC_ID, that stays in place for the life of that PDC. When you read a trace, you can match each flow to its context by that ID. Keep the ID in your notes for the whole capture, so you can tell two busy flows apart when they share the same link. The three UEC profiles must support NSCC on lossy networks. That rule matters because a lossy network is the one that most deployments start with, and NSCC is the control that keeps it usable. As of mid-2025, RCCC is optional for lossy IP fabrics.

4.1 Two-Tier Congestion Management: NSCC, RCCC (256B Credits), and Link-Level CBFC (32 VCs)​

In a traditional RoCEv2 cluster, a sudden many-to-one incast at a receiver fills the top-of-rack switch buffers. Picture many roads merging into one exit ramp at rush hour. The ramp backs up, and the cars on other roads that never touched the ramp get stuck behind the jam. That triggers Priority Flow Control pause frames, and those pauses freeze innocent victim flows that share the same link. If you run RoCEv2 today, this is the failure to watch for. A pause frame is a blunt tool, because it stops a whole link rather than the one flow that caused the problem. The victims are often unrelated jobs that happened to share the same uplink. UEC removes the PFC pause storms and head-of-line blocking by separating the two jobs. The end-to-end control slows the senders that cause the pressure, and the link-level control protects each hop without freezing unrelated traffic. End-to-end congestion control runs between endpoint NICs, and the lossless link-level control becomes optional.

UEC End-to-End (NSCC / RCCC) and Link-Level (CBFC) Quantitative Parameters:
- Network Signal Congestion Control (NSCC): sender-driven congestion window (cwnd) adjusted via RTT, ECN flag in NACKs, and ACK_CC telemetry (received byte count, service time, out-of-order packet count)
- Receiver Credit Congestion Control (RCCC): target-driven Credit Pool requested via Credit Target (req_cc_state) and granted via 24-bit ACK congestion control field in units of 256 bytes:
  256 credits x 256 bytes = 65,536 bytes (64 KiB) authorized transmission
  Maximum 24-bit RCCC credit window = (2^24 - 1) x 256 = 4,294,967,040 bytes (~4.0 GiB)
- Link-Level Credit-Based Flow Control (CBFC):
  32 Virtual Channels (VCs) per port (2x InfiniBand's 16 VLs, 4x IEEE PFC's 8 priorities)
  Discovered via LLDP; tracks Consumed Credit (CC) and Freed Credit (CF) counters per VC
  Uses modified 64B/66B PCS physical sublayer out-of-band Control Ordered Set (CtlOS) CP_Update messages at line rate

The three mechanisms act at different points on the path, and each one has a separate job. Keep the three apart in your head, because they fail in different ways. Think of them as three guards at three gates. End-to-end control watches the whole route, receiver credits watch the door of the destination, and link-level credits watch each short hop on the way.

  1. Network Signal Congestion Control: This is mandatory across all three UEC profiles on lossy fabrics. The sender PDS keeps an explicit congestion window of in-flight, unacknowledged bytes. The receiver returns ACK_CC packets that carry the received byte count, the service time, and the out-of-order packet count. It can also return NACK packets with the ECN flag. The sender adjusts cwnd using RTT and ECN feedback, and it rotates its UDP source port or PDS Entropy field.
  2. Receiver Credit Congestion Control: This is an optional, target-driven scheme built to prevent many-to-one receiver incast. Senders request credit through the Credit Target (req_cc_state) field in their data packets. The target FEP arbitrates its local Credit Pool across the active incoming sessions. It grants credits back in units of 256 bytes inside a 24-bit ACK field. If the receiver buffer pool is full, it withholds credits before packets ever enter the fabric.
  3. Link-Level Credit-Based Flow Control: Inspired by InfiniBand CBFC, UEC CBFC doubles the capacity to 32 Virtual Channels per port. Lossless and best-effort channels can then share one link. Neighboring switches and NICs discover CBFC support through LLDP, and they track Consumed Credit and Freed Credit counters per VC. Instead of spending Layer 2 frame bandwidth on credit updates, the sender PCS sublayer sends out-of-band CtlOS CP_Update messages at line rate.

Think of the NSCC window as the number of boxes you may have on a conveyor belt at once. A new box goes on only when an old one has been confirmed as received. When the belt jams, you put fewer boxes on it, and when it runs clear, you add a few back.

NSCC keeps an explicit congestion window. The sender uses window-based control to estimate its packets in flight, meaning the packets the target has not yet acknowledged. It may send while the window is larger than the bytes in flight. A packet counts as having left the network once an ACK or SACK confirms it. Until that confirmation arrives, the packet still occupies a place in the window, like a parcel that is still on the truck until the receipt comes back. NACKs may carry the ECN flag. When a flow runs slowly, start by checking whether the window is shrinking.

The feedback path runs in reverse. Information about the road travels back to the car that is driving on it, so the driver can slow down before the jam gets worse. The receiver sends NACKs, or sets and clears the ECN bit, and that feedback crosses the fabric back to the sender. The sender measures round-trip time (RTT) and uses it, together with the feedback, to resize the window. Whenever NSCC is used, the ACK or SACK must carry congestion control flags. Without those flags, the sender has no signal to adjust its window, and it will keep sending at the same rate even when the path is congested. The ACK_CC reports the bytes received, the service time, and the out-of-order packet count.

RCCC works from the receiver side, and it uses credit attribution instead of window math. Think of the receiver as a cashier who hands out a fixed number of tickets for a show. The sender can enter only with a ticket, so the room never holds more people than it can seat. A sender asks for more credit through the Credit Target field in its data packets. It may transmit whenever its local credit pool at the receiver still has credit left. The receiver returns credit through credit control packets. Because the receiver shares credit information across all senders, their combined rate cannot congest it. Each sender sees the same pool, so no sender can take more than its share while the others wait.

Picture two sender servers and one receiver server. Each sender sends credit requests through its intermediate switches. The receiver's credit pool answers with credit control messages, and those messages travel back to each sender to set its rate. The advantage is simple. Think of the receiver as the person who controls the front door of a busy shop. Each customer gets in only when a slot opens, so the shop never has more people inside than it can serve. The target knows exactly how many credits remain across its active sessions, and when none are left, none are handed out.

In RCCC, the req_cc_state field tells the target how much data the sender wants to send. The target can then decide how many credits to grant, based on what its own buffers can hold at that moment. The 24-bit ACK congestion control field carries the credits, in units of 256 bytes.

Suppose a receiver has room for one 64 KiB block and wants to tell the sender how much it can accept right now. The credit field answers that question in small units, and the arithmetic below shows the size of one grant and the largest value the field can carry.

Here is the arithmetic for one credit grant. Each credit covers 256 bytes, so 256 credits cover 65,536 bytes, which is 64 KiB of authorized transmission. The largest window a 24-bit field can express is 2^24 minus 1, multiplied by 256. That comes to 4,294,967,040 bytes, or about 4.0 GiB.

CBFC is an optional link-layer function. Think of it as a prepaid card on each link. The receiver loads the card with the space it has, and the sender spends one unit for each packet it sends. When the card runs out, the sender waits, so the receiver's buffer never overflows. It gives lossless delivery by priority on a full-duplex Ethernet link. Like RCCC, it uses credits to track free buffer space at the receiver. The difference is scope. CBFC works on one segment, such as the link between two switches in the same IP fabric. InfiniBand's CBFC inspired the UEC version, which doubles the virtual channels from 16 to 32 per port. Keep the two designs side by side when you read the comparison table.

Think of the credits as a bank balance that the sender spends and the receiver refills. Unlike a pause frame, which stops everything on a link, a credit balance tells the sender exactly how much room is left for each class of traffic.

CBFC has real advantages over standard PFC. It supports more lossless classes, allows better scheduling and load balancing, and is less sensitive to cable length and frame size. Explicit credit exchange reduces head-of-line blocking and PFC storms, and it allows more granular congestion management. The receiver creates credits from its available buffer, and the sender transmits only while it holds enough of them. The sender can wait for more credits without losing its place, which is why the link stays calm under a burst. You can think of the credits as a balance that the sender spends.

Both sides track consumed credits. The sender counts what it has spent, and the receiver counts what it has freed. The two totals must agree, so a mismatch is a clear sign that something on the link is wrong. The receiver reports freed credits once traffic leaves its input buffer. Between two UEC switches, the sending side holds per-VC queues and a credit consumer, while the receiving side has its buffer and a credit generator. Packets flow hop by hop, and credits flow back the other way. Think of a relay of buckets passed along a line, where each person hands an empty bucket back to the one behind before that person hands over another full one. The receiving switch returns credits based on its port buffer state, using dedicated buffers, a shared pool, or both. Think of dedicated buffers as a reserved lane for each class, and the shared pool as a common lane that any class can use when it is free.

CBFC tracks consumed credits (CC) and freed credits (CF) with counters. The UEC specification lists them as the counters to watch at each segment. When a link misbehaves, compare the consumed and freed totals on both ends first, because that comparison often points to the failing segment faster than a packet capture. Compared with end-to-end control, CBFC needs small link-layer changes so that credit updates can run out of band. The sender's PCS sublayer sends CtlOS CP_Update messages at line rate. Switch1 does not send an explicit credit request. The link-level exchange is quieter than that. Switch1 simply reports how much it has used, and the other end works out the rest from the totals. Instead, it reports consumed credits through the CP_Update message.

Picture a switch port that runs two threshold systems at once, one for pause frames and one for credits. Each system has its own limits, and the two limits must stay in step. The paragraph below explains why engineers avoid running both on one link.

In theory, PFC and CBFC can run on different VCs of the same link. Running two threshold systems at once gets very complicated, so CBFC is the more effective approach compared with PFC and DSCP-driven control on lossless fabrics. Before any credit exchange, LLDP confirms that both ends of the segment support CBFC. Check that LLDP result first when a link misbehaves. A link that never completed the CBFC exchange will look healthy in some counters while it quietly runs without flow control.

4.2 Fast Retransmission via DSCP Packet Trimming and Hop-by-Hop Link Layer Reliability (LLR)​

Picture a collective that stalls for a long stretch because one packet was dropped in a brief burst of traffic. The whole group waits for the sender to notice the loss and resend it. UEC shortens that wait by repairing the loss closer to where it happened.

When microbursts overflow a switch buffer despite NSCC and RCCC, waiting for a sender retransmission timeout stalls the whole collective. UEC avoids that wait by catching packet drops at the switch buffer and on the physical link. Two mechanisms do the work. You will see both in the failure modes later in the chapter.

  • DSCP packet trimming: Packets in trim-eligible DSCP classes are never silently dropped when an intermediate switch buffer fills. The congested switch slices off the data payload and keeps the packet header and the original ECN-capable state. It forwards the lightweight trimmed header over a high-priority queue to the destination FEP. When the destination NIC gets a trimmed packet, it skips writing the payload into GPU HBM. It immediately sends a targeted NACK or SACK to the sender for that exact PSN. You get that fast repair without waiting for a timeout.
  • Link Layer Reliability: This is negotiated per link segment, between adjacent switches or between a NIC and a switch. Each LLR-eligible Ethernet frame receives a link-layer sequence number, SEQ, embedded in the MAC preamble. A copy goes into an on-ASIC replay buffer guarded by a short link timer.
Hop-by-Hop Link Layer Reliability (LLR) Replay Sequence (Switch 1 <-> Switch 2):
- Clean arrival (SEQ = 1): Switch 2 receives SEQ = 1 cleanly and returns link-level ACK -> Switch 1 flushes SEQ = 1 from its replay buffer
- Wire corruption or loss (SEQ = 2 or SEQ = 3): if SEQ = 2 times out, or if SEQ = 3 is lost and SEQ = 4 arrives out of order, Switch 2 immediately returns NACK SEQ = 3 -> Switch 1 retransmits the missing frame from its local ASIC replay buffer in hundreds of nanoseconds, hiding the optical bit error from end-to-end UET PDS

The PSN sequence numbers already guarantee reliable transport end to end. Think of a PSN as a page number on a letter. The receiver can tell which page is missing, and it can ask for only that page again. The trouble is that, in a large fabric, every drop forces the original sender to retransmit, and that delay can hold up the whole AI job. LLR helps by managing retransmission between neighboring nodes instead of between end hosts.

Think of LLR as a handshake between two neighbors who agree to watch each other's mail. Both sides must say yes before the watching starts, which is why the capability is advertised first.

The LLR capability is advertised between two switches, or between a host NIC and a switch. The LLR state machine runs only when both nodes agree. If only one side supports it, LLR is ignored, and normal end-to-end PSN and ACK, NACK, and SACK reliability takes over. When LLR is active, the two levels of reliability work together, which gives double-level control. The result should be a better job completion time, especially where congestion risk is high. Measure job completion time before and after, because a feature that looks good in theory can still add delay on a link that was already clean.

LLR has to live in the ASIC so that it does not slow end-to-end traffic. A chip-level replay buffer can answer a neighbor's request in hundreds of nanoseconds, while a software path would take far longer. That speed is the reason LLR is built into the silicon rather than the software. If the MAC client does not want LLR, or the frame is not eligible, the frame goes out as a standard Ethernet frame. An eligible frame gets a sequence number and a copy in a replay buffer, in case the link partner does not receive it. Here is how a lossy link recovers. Switch1 sends SEQ 1 and stores a copy with a timer running. Switch2 receives it and returns an ACK, so Switch1 clears its copy.

Next, SEQ 2 is lost in transit. The timer expires, and Switch1 resends it. Then SEQ 3 is lost while SEQ 4 arrives first. Switch2 sees the gap and sends NACK SEQ 3, so Switch1 resends the missing frame. The sender never has to involve the end hosts for that repair. That is the part to remember: the repair stays between two neighbors. The end hosts never see the lost frame, so their timers and their windows keep running as if nothing happened at that hop.

LLR is still optional. Think of it as a second safety net under the first one. Operators can add it on the links where loss is most costly, and leave it off where the link is short and clean. It complements RUD, ROD, and RUDI retransmissions with PSN-based recovery. Retransmission happens at the link level instead of at the server level, so for the PDC the LLR step can stay transparent. LLR suits latency-sensitive AI work, such as inference on GPU servers, where segment-level control may give a positive outcome. You decide whether to enable it per link, so plan the links one at a time. Enable it on one link, measure the result, and only then move to the next link. A change made everywhere at once makes the cause of any new problem hard to find.

4.3 In-Network Collectives (INC) vs User-Space xCCL, Management, Interoperability, and Compliance​

Under the UEC Software Working Group, the Libfabric fi_collective API bridges PyTorch, Open MPI, MPICH (MPI_Allreduce, MPI_Alltoall), and vendor xCCLs to switch-assisted In-Network Collectives:

  • Switch-assisted INC execution: An out-of-band INC Manager talks to INC Agents running inside sFEPs over gRPC. For collective groups (Collectives-gr1, Collectives-gr2), leaf INC agents forward vectors to an elected root-of-tree spine switch (Spine 1 or Spine 2). That root spine switch aggregates or replicates the collective flow once in hardware, then distributes the result downstream.
  • Management, interoperability, and certification: UET exports per-flow telemetry that tracks CBFC and RCCC credits, latency, jitter, and drops across Libfabric, MPI, NCCL, RCCL, and the INC Manager. For backward compatibility, UEC Specification v1.0 supports dual-mode link negotiation, so brownfield IP fabrics can run lossless RoCEv2 alongside end-to-end UET. Link-level CBFC and LLR are reserved for greenfield deployments. The UEC compliance suite validates multi-vendor interoperability, line-rate performance, and TSS security across the AI Base, AI Full, and HPC profiles.

INC refers to compute work done inside the network. Picture a relay where a switch takes several small envelopes from its neighbors, combines them into one, and passes that single envelope along. The work is the same sum, but far fewer envelopes cross the links. A common example is aggregation for AllReduce, an idea first introduced in NVIDIA's SHARP framework. INC supports an xCCL with network-level optimizations, such as data transformation, filtering, and in-network routing decisions tuned to each collective. Grouping a collective's flows onto specific switches and servers improves GPU use and latency.

Hierarchical aggregation at a switch, such as a spine, cuts data copies and data movement. Think of a regional post office that sorts the mail before it goes to the local branches, so fewer bags travel the long route. Here is the practical view. Think of the stack as a set of translators. Each one passes the request to the next, and each one must understand the message it receives before it can pass it on. PyTorch calls down into MPI, and MPI calls Libfabric. Libfabric sends the collective request both to the INC manager and to the network fabric. The INC manager reaches the switch agents over an out-of-band control path using gRPC. If you debug INC, start with the manager and work down to the agents.

Two collective groups, Collectives-gr1 and Collectives-gr2, each hold their own leaf agents and root-of-tree spine agents. The sFEP, an Ethernet switch that runs UEC and INC, has the INC engine built into the silicon's microkernel. The INC manager (IM) handles control and coordination for these agents. It decides which switch is the root of each tree, and it does not carry the data itself. That split keeps the control path separate from the data path.

Take one simple case. Picture a result that one server must share with two others. The naive path sends two full copies, and the smarter path sends one copy to a switch that does the fan-out. The second path uses less bandwidth on the link that is most likely to be busy. GPU-server1 needs to send the result of its local computation to GPU-server2 and GPU-server3. Instead of sending two copies, it sends one copy to the spine elected as the INC tree root. That root distributes the result to the rest of the switches in the group. Bandwidth is saved between leaf and spine, and latency is more even across the servers. Consider the same case with your own server names. Write the path on paper, with each hop labeled, and mark the point where the copy count drops. That mark is where the saving happens.

5. Scale-Up Systems: Architecture, OCP SUE-T (ESUN), UALink 1.0, and Memory Coherence​

5.1 Why Scale-Up Pods Exist: Latency, Bandwidth, and the 4-Rack / 1,000-xPU Boundary​

Picture a conversation that must go back and forth thousands of times per second between a small group of accelerators. Every reply waits for the one before it, so a delay of a few hundred nanoseconds becomes a visible slowdown. That is the problem scale-up fabrics are built to solve.

Scale-out networks connect independent servers across multi-tier leaf-spine fabrics, using external 400G or 800G NICs over PCIe. Seven latency-critical AI workloads, however, exchange activations at a sub-microsecond cadence. Picture each workload as a conversation that cannot pause for long. A reply that arrives late stalls the next step, and the accelerator waits with nothing to do. They include foundational LLM training, multimodal text-to-video diffusion, Mixture-of-Experts token routing, and multi-step reasoning. They also include digital-twin physics simulation, Graph Neural Networks with sparse neighbor reads, nanosecond financial risk modeling, and edge federated inference. External NICs and PCIe buses cannot feed these workloads without stalling the accelerators. Consider one of these workloads, and you can see why the interconnect matters for it. A model that spends most of its time waiting on a neighbor is limited by the wire, however fast the chips are.

Scale-Up Interconnect vs PCIe / CXL Latency, Bandwidth, and Pod Geometry Specs:
- PCIe 5.0: 500 ns to 1 us latency, 32 GT/s per lane, 3.94 GB/s per lane (64 Gbps / 63.02 GB/s unidirectional on x16)
- PCIe 6.0: 8 GB/s per lane (~128 GB/s / ~128 Gbps bidirectional on x16)
- CXL 3.0: ~100 ns cache-coherent shared memory latency, tied to PCIe 6.0 (32 GT/s) per-lane bandwidth
- NVIDIA NVLink 5 (NVL72): 100-300 ns latency, 1.8 Tbps (1,800 Gbps) bidirectional per GPU across 18 NVLink switches connecting 72 GPUs (B300 compute tray packs 4 GPUs with 1,152 GB aggregate HBM3)
- Scale-up workload examples: text-to-video diffusion spreading 1,024 images across 512 xPUs at 2 images per micro-batch; GNN irregular sparse reads at ~300 ns
- Physical scale-up pod geometry: 72 to 1,000 xPUs across 1 to 4 pre-engineered racks (pods), < 4 m in-rack copper/optical cables, 200 Gbps SerDes lanes from on-die interconnect points (IP) into single-hop scale-up switches (ESUN/SUE-T, UALink Switches ULS, or NVSwitch) at 200-300 ns port-to-port latency (< 1 us end-to-end RTT)
- Horizontal pod-to-pod scale-out: separate 400G/800G scale-out NICs over PCIe Gen5/Gen6 across multi-plane Fabric Plane 1 | Plane 2 Clos networks

Across dense accelerator scale-up systems, the main goal is simpler and faster memory access. Think of the goal as a shared whiteboard that every team member can write on directly, instead of passing notes across the room. Engineers want a large model to look like one memory space that every accelerator can reach directly. That keeps the software stack smaller. The high-bandwidth memory (HBM) on many accelerators then reads as one coherent pool. Moving data from one accelerator to another also carries less overhead. There is no IP or UDP header to add, and no multi-stage network to cross. The path becomes an optimized Ethernet switch system with one hop. For a large language model, the aim is a more direct memory load semantic, and a faster one. Each xPU sees a more coherent multi-xPU high-bandwidth memory view. A tensor that one accelerator writes can be read by another without a separate copy step, which removes a layer of bookkeeping from the training code. Less fabric interconnect setup is needed, and the software stack stays simple. For you as a designer, that means fewer moving parts to configure.

Multimodal models form another use case. Picture a diffusion model that runs many small denoising steps, and syncs its gradients after each batch of images. The sync cost repeats thousands of times, so the interconnect cost is paid again and again. Text-to-video, audio, and 3D generators rely on diffusion models. These models move large tensors between GPUs, and they must sync often. That exchange can repeat thousands of times for each batch. Here is a concrete case. A batch of 1,024 images spreads across 512 xPUs. Each xPU handles two images as a micro-batch. After that, the xPUs sync their gradients across the interconnect point for one step. The sync runs over the whole shared memory, so the model behaves as if it trained on a single xPU. You can picture the whole pod as one large accelerator from the software side.

Work the numbers yourself. The point is to see how a large batch divides cleanly across the pod, so that each accelerator has a fair share of the work before the sync step. Each xPU holds 2 images, and 512 xPUs hold 1,024 images in total, so one step covers the full batch. Every xPU then syncs its gradients once across the shared memory.

Financial risk analysis is another case. Picture a risk model that must refresh its exposure numbers as the market moves. A slow shared memory view means stale numbers, and stale numbers are a cost the business feels right away. Traders and analysts need to see market volatility and their exposure quickly. Real-time market models keep updating with fresh financial data. High-frequency algorithms use that data to predict trends, tune trading strategies, and manage risk as the economy changes. What ties these cases together is the need for many accelerators to share one fast memory view. Whatever your workload, that is the question to ask first.

5.2 Open Compute Project (OCP) Scale-Up Ethernet Transport (SUE-T / ESUN)​

Picture a tiny message that carries more header than data. Inside a rack, that ratio wastes wire time on every single transfer, and the waste adds up across millions of small loads and stores. The scale-up transport was designed to remove that waste.

Standard Ethernet frames carry tens of bytes of IP, UDP, and RDMA headers. On the small 64-byte or 256-byte memory loads and stores inside a rack, those headers waste wire bandwidth. Announced in October 2025 under the OCP Ethernet Scale-Up Networking initiative, Scale-Up Ethernet Transport adapts standard 100 Gbps and 200 Gbps PAM-4 Ethernet PHY and MAC silicon for single-hop scale-up pods. It replaces IP, UDP, and BTH with two compact Layer 2 headers. Think of the swap as trading a long address label for a short sticker that carries only what the switch needs to route the frame inside the rack.

OCP SUE-T (ESUN) Framing, Header Efficiency, and On-Die Interface Specifications:
- Physical interface scaling: 100G/200G PAM-4 SerDes (800G, 1.6T, or 3.2T per SUE interface; e.g., 12 SUE instances x 12 lanes per xPU connected 1:1 to 12 ESUN switches, ~250 ns switch hop latency)
- Base frame and PDU sizes: converts a 256-byte frame into a scale-up flit carrying up to a 4,096-byte SUE PDU over a Virtual Channel (VC)
- AI Fabric Header (AFH Generation 2):
  1. Compressed AFH (6 bytes): for single-hop scale-up pods; encodes Destination xPU, Traffic Class, Source xPU, Opaque bits, and control flags (V version 0, W compressed vs non-compressed format/hop count, Z administratively assigned local MAC identifier, X/Y/R reserved, M multicast) inside Ethernet DMAC/SMAC positions
     -> 256B payload + 6B AFH Gen 2 + 2B EtherType + 8B RH + 8B R-CRC/FCS = 280 bytes total (91.43% payload efficiency)
  2. Non-Compressed AFH (12 bytes): for two-hop scale-up topologies
     -> 256B payload + 12B AFH Gen 2 + 2B EtherType + 8B RH + 8B R-CRC/FCS = 286 bytes total (89.51% payload efficiency)
- Reliability Header (RH, 8 bytes after EtherType, before SUE Payload + R-CRC + PAD + FCS):
  Row 1: VER, OP (opcode), RSV, 10-bit xPU ID (up to 1,024 xPUs), 16-bit NPSN (Next/Packet Sequence Number)
  Row 2: VC (4 or more virtual channels), RSV, Partition (multi-tenant virtual pod isolation), 16-bit APSN (Acknowledge Sequence Number)
- Two On-Die xPU-to-SUE Interfaces:
  1. FIFO Interface: 1 raw PDU stream using a 10-bit xPU identifier + 2-bit VC identifier
  2. ARM AXI 4.0 Interface: 5 independent memory transaction channels mapped across 2 VCs:
     - Requests on VC0: AR (Read Address), AW (Write Address), W (Write Data)
     - Responses on VC1: B (Write Response), R (Read Data)

The header choice comes down to two cases, and the efficiency numbers make the difference concrete. Think of the header as the envelope around a letter. A thinner envelope leaves more room for the letter itself, and the percentages show how much of each frame is letter and how much is envelope. Take a 256-byte payload. With the 6-byte compressed header, the frame is 280 bytes, and the payload is 91.43% of it. With the 12-byte non-compressed header for two-hop topologies, the frame is 286 bytes, and the payload is 89.51%. The compressed form is 6 bytes shorter, and the chapter pairs it with single-hop pods. If your pod has two hops, you need the 12-byte header instead.

The SUE-T design rests on three pieces, and each one is worth a closer look. Each piece answers one question. How is the frame addressed, how is it kept reliable, and how does the accelerator hand it over?

  1. AI Fabric Header Generation 2: It replaces standard MAC addressing with a 6-byte compressed header for single-hop pods, or a 12-byte non-compressed header for two-hop topologies. Both formats keep IEEE Ethernet frame compatibility.
  2. Reliability Header and dual-layer loss recovery: The 8-byte Reliability Header sits right after the EtherType. It tracks end-to-end sequence numbers, NPSN and APSN, across up to 1,024 xPUs. Hop-by-hop Link Layer Reliability retransmits lost frames from the ESUN switch replay buffer, and link-level CBFC prevents buffer overflow.
  3. On-die FIFO and ARM AXI 4.0 interfaces: Between the xPU compute core and the on-die SUE transport block, designers choose either a raw streaming FIFO interface or a 5-channel ARM AXI 4.0 memory interface. The AXI 4.0 interface keeps read and write requests (AR, AW, W) on VC0, separate from read and write responses (B, R) on VC1. You should keep that split in mind when you read a trace.

5.3 Ultra Accelerator Link (UALink 1.0): 6.4 Tbps Stations, 64B/640B Flits, UPLI, and GVA -> NPA -> SPA​

Suppose a message must cross several switches between two accelerators. Each switch that waits for a full variable-length frame adds a little delay, and those small delays stack up. The UALink design attacks that stacking at its source.

Variable-length Ethernet frames and standard 4-way Reed-Solomon FEC codeword interleaving add hundreds of nanoseconds of delay at every switch hop. The Ultra Accelerator Link Specification 1.0 removes variable-length Ethernet framing altogether. It connects up to 1,000 accelerators across single-hop UALink Switches (ULS 0..31) or direct full-mesh UALink Direct links. Every transfer uses fixed-size flits that align 1:1 with physical PCS codewords. If you come from Ethernet, this is the biggest change you will notice. Ethernet engineers are used to thinking in packets and MAC addresses. In this protocol, the unit is a fixed-size flit and the address is a memory location, so the mental model must shift.

5.4 Hardware Directory Coherence vs Software PGAS Coherence and Scale-Up Comparison​

Assume that a multi-GPU kernel will behave the same on an open pod as it does on NVIDIA's NVL72 rack, and you can ship silent stale-memory bugs. When an engineer ports such a kernel from NVL72 to an open UALink or SUE-T pod, that assumption is the first thing to check. A fundamental architectural divide separates proprietary hardware-coherent pods from open software-coherent scale-up pods. In a hardware-coherent pod, the fabric keeps every cache copy up to date for you. In a software-coherent pod, your code must tell the fabric when a copy is safe to read. You need to know which kind of pod you run before you port any code.

  1. Hardware cache coherence: On NVLink and NVSwitch, AMD Infinity Fabric, and CXL 3.0, the fabric silicon enforces cache consistency automatically, without software intervention. A directory-based home agent tracks which xPU caches hold copies of each cache line. When GPU 1 writes to memory address X, the NVSwitch sends hardware invalidate snoop messages to all peer GPUs that cache line X. It collects the acknowledgments and then grants exclusive ownership to GPU 1. Hardware coherence allows transparent pointer dereferencing across 72 GPUs in NVL72. But snoop directory traffic scales poorly toward 1,000 accelerators, and it locks the pod to one vendor. You get transparent pointers, but you pay for it in scale and lock-in. Think of hardware coherence as a very attentive assistant who watches every room. The assistant works well for a small house, but the cost of watching grows fast as the house gets bigger.
  2. Software-managed PGAS coherence: To scale cleanly to 1,000 multi-vendor accelerators, UALink 1.0 and SUE-T leave out hardware cache snoops. They adopt a Partitioned Global Address Space memory model above the Transaction Layer. Runtime libraries such as NCCL, RCCL, SHMEM, CUDA, and ROCm explicitly coordinate cache flushes and memory visibility at collective boundaries. They do this with lightweight TL control verbs, RMA_FENCE, RMA_SYNC, NOTIFY, RMA_COMPLETION, and ATOMIC_ACK. So when you port code to these pods, you add those barriers yourself.

5.5 Pod Building Blocks, HBM Placement, and Vendor Scope​

A scale-up pod is not assembled part by part on the data center floor. Think of the pod as a prebuilt appliance that arrives with its wiring already done. Your job is to place it, power it, and connect its uplinks, which is far less work than building a fabric from parts. Vendors build the compute system with its parts already fitted into the rack. The chassis, its intra-chassis cabling, the optics, the management switches, and the storage units all arrive prebuilt. The buyer gets one management interface that covers the accelerators, the compute, and the storage. The options a buyer can change are limited. You should ask the vendor early which of those options you need. A late request for a different storage layout or a different NIC count can delay the whole rack by weeks.

Picture a buyer who wants to add a second vendor's pod to an existing one. The question is whether the two chassis can share the same fabric. The answer, for most current products, is that the vendors do not design for that mix, so the buyer should plan separate pods.

Interoperability between two vendors' scale-up chassis is not typically addressed. A common deployment uses one to four pods from the same vendor. Keeping one vendor inside the pod keeps the management plane, the firmware, and the support contract simple, which is worth more than a small saving on hardware. Those pods then connect to the existing scale-out systems. Think of the pods as large appliances that plug into the building's main supply, rather than as separate buildings that must talk to each other directly. If you plan a mixed-vendor design, check this yourself. Figure 13-1 shows four identical pods joined by a rack-to-rack link, which can use scale-out or scale-up extenders. The top bar is labeled Rack to Rack Interconnection, with Scale out or Scale up Extenders. From top to bottom, each pod holds out-of-band (OOB) management switches, power shelves, compute, and accelerators. Below them sits a boundary of interconnect switches, such as ESUN, UALink, or NVLink switches. Under that boundary come another accelerator block, compute, NVMe-oF storage, and power shelves. A three-tier IP Clos scale-out fabric can connect even more racks.

A four-pod or four-rack scale-up system can hold from 72 to hundreds of xPUs. Picture the pod as one large room that holds many workers, where any worker can reach any tool in the room without walking through a door. Those xPUs can be GPUs, TPUs, or other accelerators. Each accelerator has its own HBM, placed physically next to it. The HBM is still exposed as part of one coherent shared memory to every other xPU. Each HBM module holds hundreds of gigabytes. Memory software manages the HBM, usually at the compute level. The compute host connects over PCIe, much like a multi-core CPU or ARM chip. When SUE or UALink switches carry the AI training traffic, that PCIe link is not used for it. So you do not need to route training traffic over the host bus.

5.6 Single-Hop Mesh Wiring and Attaching Pods to Fabric Planes​

Picture a rack where any two accelerators can talk without passing a second switch. Every pair is one hop apart, so the latency between any two of them is the same. That property is useful for a model that must exchange data with every peer.

Inside the rack, the wiring forms a full mesh that is only one hop wide. Figure 13-3 shows 64 accelerators, ACC1 through ACC64. A rounded box labeled Accelerators sits at the top, with a dashed range standing in for ACC2 through ACC63. Twelve scale-up switches are enabled in that rack. Each accelerator has twelve connections, labeled SUE T 1 through SUE T 12, one to each switch, so every accelerator reaches every switch in a crisscross full mesh. Each lane runs at 100 Gbps or 200 Gbps into the SUE-T switch. Any two accelerators are one hop apart, which keeps latency low for AI workloads. A design where every pair is one hop apart is easier to reason about, because the path length does not depend on which two accelerators talk. You get that property from the wiring alone, not from software.

The physical Ethernet connection stays the same. SUE-T adds the new AFH transport and an optimized Ethernet header to carry remote memory access from one accelerator to another. The RoCEv2 and UET frame formats are not used here, because they add overhead. A single-hop design needs less entropy handling, so the RoCEv2 and BTH fields are not needed. The memory access rules are also simpler, which shortens the time needed to write results into a remote HBM.

A scale-up rack still has to reach the rest of the AI data center. Each compute system uses a dedicated scale-out NIC for that link. Figure 13-4 shows two fabric planes, and production deployments use more planes for better availability. Scale-up System 1 has two NICs. The left NIC receives 800 Gbps from Plane 1, and the right NIC receives 800 Gbps from Plane 2. Each accelerator has an 800 Gbps link that splits into two 400 Gbps links, one to each of two SUE switches. When you cable a rack, count those links on both sides.

A multi-plane design offers faster convergence and a controlled minimum of compute capacity across several scale-up systems. Think of two bridges across the same river. If one bridge closes for repairs, the other one still carries traffic, although each car may need a slower route. If one plane goes offline, the capacity between scale-up systems still equals one plane plus the compute capacity of each system. The number of 800 Gbps NIC ports per compute node may vary from two to four with 800 Gbps cards. With 400 Gbps cards, the count runs from four to eight. Those ports connect to ToR Ethernet switches on different fabric planes. A single-plane fabric with inter-pod links also works. Pick the plane count that matches the availability you need. It supports the path diversity options from Chapter 8, IP Routing for AI/ML Fabrics.

5.7 Full Scale-Up Comparison and Why Open Standards Matter​

Comparing NVLink with the open fabrics takes care. Public documentation is thinner for NVLink, while the open standards publish their framing and address rules in detail. A fair comparison weighs what each vendor has published, not only what the marketing page claims. Public NVLink documentation gives fewer detailed specifications than the open standards do. SUE-T was announced as a framework, so its details may change over time. UALink is the best-defined of the three. A well-defined specification gives engineers something concrete to test against, so the gaps between a product and the document are easier to spot. Its transaction and data link flits over the Ethernet PHY, its request semantics, and its address translation are all specified. 5.8 UALink Flit Layout, Station Wiring, and Address Handles​

UALink moves data in small fixed units, and its addresses are not plain MAC addresses. Think of the fixed units as standard shipping containers. Every container has the same size, so the terminal can plan the space for each one before it arrives. Readers who expect Ethernet behavior often get confused here. The next pieces show how one write request is packed, wired, and addressed. Keep the Ethernet picture aside while you read them. Think of the UALink format as a different language with its own grammar. Translating each word into Ethernet terms only causes confusion.

Figure 13-13 shows how one uncompressed write request fits into a 64-byte TL flit. The flit has an upper half and a lower half, each 32 bytes. In the upper half, the data pieces Req0.Data.0, Req0.Data.2, and Req0.Data.4 stack on top of each other. The lower half starts with four request-control slots, Req3, Req2, Req1, and Req0. The pieces Req0.Data.1 and Req0.Data.3 sit on the right side of the lower half. So the data for one request spreads across both halves of the flit.

Figure 13-10 shows how accelerators wire into the UALink switches in one rack. Each accelerator has eight connection points, Station 0 through Station 7. Each station holds four 200 Gbps lanes. So each accelerator has 32 lanes and 6.4 Tbps of bandwidth. Station 0 connects to ULS 0 and ULS 1. Station 7 connects to ULS 30 and ULS 31. Each station fans out to several switches, and the same pattern repeats for every accelerator.

The software layer registers memory regions on each accelerator. Registration is the step where the runtime tells the hardware which memory may be reached from outside, and it is done before any transfer starts. It defines local SPA ranges and exports them as an address handle table. The handle value goes into the UPLI request, next to the target accelerator identifier. The UALink switch reads the 10-bit accelerator ID and picks the outgoing interface. Ethernet MAC addresses play no role in that step. The PHY is still Ethernet, but the switch routes on a memory-mapped scheme instead. You will see this when you trace a request through the switch.

The address handle is a reference object. Think of it as a labeled key card for one room in a building. The card does not open the room by itself. It tells the access system which room to open, and which hardware path to use to get there. It holds everything needed to reach a remote memory region over UALink, including the hardware context and the access control for remote memory access. The originator uses the handle ID in its UPLI requests. The UALink hardware swaps that ID for the NPA plus key bits before anything goes on the wire. A page table entry in the accelerator's MMU links the handle to the accelerator identifier.

On the request side, a 10-bit DSTACCID field maps into the NPA address. The SRCACCID field lets the responder build the return NPA address. At the destination, the TL decoder takes the delivered flit from UPLI and the MMU. It queues the flit and creates the right receive memory cache entry.

Figure 13-15 shows the three address spaces from end to end. On the source side, three CU blocks send requests to one accelerator MMU, all in the guest virtual address space. The middle section holds the UALink switches, which work in the network physical address space. On the destination side, the second accelerator's MMU translates the address to a system physical address, which reaches two HBM blocks. Follow the arrows from source to destination, and you have the whole translation.

Common first mistakes​

These four mistakes come up again and again in storage and UEC reviews. Each one looks reasonable on a slide, and each one breaks a real fabric. Think of them as the common potholes on a new road, and check each one before you drive the first truck over the surface. Each one looks reasonable on a slide, and each one breaks a real fabric. Read them before you draw the first diagram, and you will avoid the most common traps.

  1. Confusing NSID with PRP1, PRP2, and SGL in NVMe-oF packet captures. The NSID picks the target logical volume on the storage array controller. PRP1 and PRP2 pointers, and SGL descriptors, do a different job. They name the initiator host or GPU BAR1 buffer addresses where DMA places the data. If you read one as the other, the capture will not make sense. Keep a short note beside your capture that labels each field, so the next reader does not repeat the same confusion.
  2. Sizing storage fabrics at 1:1 non-blocking capacity, or stretching RoCEv2 across 40 km+ links. Dedicated storage fabrics run efficiently at 1:3 leaf-to-spine oversubscription, provided that the 100G or 200G storage NICs sit on Fab A and Fab B with symmetric failover capacity. Across inter-site links of 40 km+, never stretch lossless RoCEv2 and PFC. Use NVMe-o-TCP with deep-buffer switches, or HTTPS S3 tiering, instead. If you plan a link like that, expect a different transport from the one you use inside the site. The inside of the site and the link between sites have different loss and delay profiles, and each one needs its own transport choice.
  3. Configuring switches to hash UET packets on RoCEv2 BTH Queue Pair offsets. UET removes the InfiniBand Base Transport Header completely, so a BTH-based hash has nothing to read. Switches that forward UET traffic must hash on the UDP Source Port for UET-over-UDP port 49150. For UET-over-IP protocol 253, they hash on the PDS Entropy field. With unencrypted SES fields, they can also hash on those. Check which fields your switches can see before you pick the hash, because the choice decides whether the load spreads across all links or piles onto one.
  4. Assuming standard Ethernet switches can transparently switch UALink 1.0 traffic. UALink 1.0 reuses 200G IEEE 802.3 PHY signaling, but it aligns 640-byte DL flits directly to RS(544, 514) PCS codewords with 1-way or 2-way interleaving. It also switches on the 10-bit ACC-ID and NPA memory addresses, not on Ethernet MAC headers. That requires purpose-built UALink Switches. A standard Ethernet switch looks at the MAC header, and that header is not where a UALink switch finds its route, so the swap is not a simple firmware change. OCP SUE-T under ESUN is different, because it keeps IEEE-compliant DMAC and SMAC positions through its 6-byte or 12-byte AFH Gen 2 header.

  1. "Walk me through the exact wire-level difference between establishing an NVMe-o-TCP session and an NVMe-o-RDMA (RoCEv2) session."
    • What they are testing: Depth of storage protocol state machines beyond high-level TCP versus RDMA summaries. Your answer should show that you know the steps, not only the names.
    • Senior answer: NVMe-o-TCP runs entirely over TCP port 8009 on a lossy IP fabric. After the TCP handshake and optional TLS, the host exchanges ICReq and ICResp PDUs and NVMe Connect requests to bind a 1:1 SQ and CQ pair. It then transfers CapsuleCmd, H2CData or C2HData, and CapsuleResp PDUs, and it packs multiple PDUs into each MTU 9000 TCP segment. The steps are the same whether you read a capture or answer on a whiteboard. NVMe-o-RDMA over RoCEv2 is different. It needs a lossless fabric at 1:3 oversubscription, and it spans three ports. First, TCP port 8009 serves the Discovery Controller Get Log Page, which returns RDMA_PRTYPE = 04 for RoCEv2. Second, UDP port 4420 handles the RDMA CM handshake that brings the QPair to RTS. Third, UDP port 4791 handles the 1:1 queue creation, the RDMA_SEND command capsules, and the controller-initiated RDMA_READ and RDMA_WRITE bulk DMA.
  2. "How does Ultra Ethernet (UEC v1.0) eliminate RoCEv2's PFC storms and Go-Back-N retransmission penalties?"
    • What they are testing: Understanding of UET PDS and SES, delivery modes, NSCC and RCCC, packet trimming, and LLR.
    • Senior answer: RoCEv2 pins flows to static BTH QPairs, and it flushes an entire window through Go-Back-N when a single packet drops. That forces operators to rely on hop-by-hop PFC pause frames. UEC v1.0 replaces the BTH with UET over UDP port 49150 or IP protocol 253, split into PDS and SES. First, RUD and RUDI allow out-of-order Direct Data Placement into GPU HBM, with selective retransmission of only the missing PSN. Second, sender-driven NSCC and target-driven RCCC, which uses 256-byte receiver credits in a 24-bit ACK field, prevent congestion and receiver incast end to end, without PFC. Third, DSCP packet trimming slices off payloads on congested switches. It forwards the trimmed headers on a high-priority queue, which triggers immediate targeted NACKs. Optional link-level LLR and 32-VC CBFC handle hop-by-hop loss and flow control cleanly. Name the three mechanisms in order, and the answer will sound complete.
  3. "Compare NVIDIA NVLink, OCP SUE-T (ESUN), and UALink 1.0 for building a scale-up super-accelerator pod."

    • Senior answer: NVLink 5 in NVL72 connects 72 GPUs across 18 proprietary NVSwitches, at 1.8 Tbps per GPU and 100 to 300 ns latency. It uses hardware directory and snoop cache coherence. Checkpoint questions (20)​

1. How do local PCIe Gen5 SSD latency, InfiniBand storage switch latency, and Ethernet storage switch latency compare?

You can read the three latencies as a ladder. A local PCIe Gen5 SSD answers in roughly 100 ns. A native InfiniBand storage switch adds roughly 300 ns. An Ethernet storage switch adds roughly 700 to 900 ns of port-to-port latency.

2. What are the four AI storage network topology designs, and what leaf-to-spine oversubscription ratio is typical for dedicated storage fabrics?

You can choose among four designs: physical Fab A and Fab B separation, logical Fab A and Fab B separation with EVPN-VXLAN RT5 or MAC-VRF, a collapsed Fab A and Fab B spine, and a hybrid on-prem plus multi-cloud fabric. Dedicated storage fabrics typically run at 1:3 leaf-to-spine oversubscription.

3. Why is NVMe-o-TCP preferred over NVMe-o-RDMA (RoCEv2) for inter-site storage replication across 40 km+ links?

Keeping RoCEv2 lossless with DCQCN and PFC over a 40 km+ link is fragile. The long round-trip delay exhausts the buffer headroom. NVMe-o-TCP runs reliably over a lossy IP core, using standard TCP congestion and flow control together with deep-buffer switches. You can use that reasoning in any design review.

4. Trace the complete NVMe-o-TCP session establishment, data transfer, and teardown sequence on TCP port 8009.

Consider the handshake first, because every later step depends on it. Each step must finish before the next one can start. 1. Complete the TCP SYN -> SYN-ACK -> ACK handshake on port 8009, plus the optional TLS handshake. 2. Exchange an NVMe/TCP ICReq PDU and an ICResp PDU, which set the host and controller capabilities, the digests, and the data offset. 3. Exchange a NVMe/TCP Connect Request and a Connect Response, which bind the controller to the SQ and CQ 1:1. 4. Move the data with CapsuleCmd (0x01), H2CData (0x03) or C2HData (0x04), and CapsuleResp (0x02). Reads use opcode 0x02, and writes use 0x01. 5. Tear the session down gracefully with H2CTermReq or C2HTermReq.

5. What are the six ordered fields of an NVMe-o-TCP PDU, and how does MTU 9000 improve PDU transport efficiency?

In a capture, you can check that the fields arrive in this order. Each NVMe-o-TCP PDU is built in this order: Common Header, then PDU-Specific Header, then Header Digest, then PDU Padding, then PDU Data, and finally Data Digest. If you configure MTU 9000 end to end across the storage fabric, two or more NVMe-o-TCP PDUs can share one TCP segment.

6. Trace the three-phase NVMe-o-RDMA (RoCEv2) session state machine and list the three RDMA_PRTYPE provider codes.
  1. Discovery: Over TCP port 8009, the host sends Get Log Page – Discovery Log Pages. The target returns RDMA_PRTYPE 04 for RoCEv2, 02 for InfiniBand, or 05 for iWARP.
  2. RDMA CM: Over UDP port 4420, the endpoints exchange RDMA_CM_REQUEST, then RDMA_CM_REPLY, then RDMA_CM_RTU. The QPair enters RTS at that point.
  3. NVMe connect and data: Over UDP port 4791, the session runs the NVMe RDMA Connect Capsule and the NVMe IO Queue Create. The controller then starts the RDMA_READ and RDMA_WRITE transfers, which ride on BTH and RETH.
7. How does native InfiniBand storage handle addressing, inter-subnet routing, frame integrity, and per-hop lossless QoS?

You will find that the Subnet Manager gives each node a 16-bit LID, which sits in the Local Routing Header, and a subnet can hold up to 48,000 nodes. Larger or multi-subnet fabrics route through the optional Global Routing Header. Each frame runs LRH -> GRH -> BTH -> ETH -> Payload -> ICRC + VCRC. Lossless QoS works across up to 16 Virtual Lanes, and each 64-byte CBFC credit in the LLH paces the traffic.

8. What are the three UEC Specification v1.0 service profiles, and how does Libfabric map application requests into PDS and SES?

The three profiles are AI Base, AI Full, and HPC. An application calls Libfabric through fi_mr and fi_collective. Libfabric turns semantic operations such as JobID, PIDonFEP, Resource Index, Buffer Offset, and Message ID into the Semantic Sublayer. The Packet Delivery Sublayer then receives the delivery and reliability rules, which cover SPDCID and DPDCID, PSN, the delivery mode, and NSCC and RCCC.

9. Compare UET-over-UDP and UET-over-IP encapsulations across protocol numbers, ports, entropy fields, and switch parsing limits.

If you compare them, Read the two encapsulations as two ways to carry the same UET packet. UET-over-UDP rides inside IP Protocol 17 with UDP destination port 49150, and it carries its path-spraying entropy in the UDP source port. UET-over-IP drops the UDP header, uses IP Protocol 253, and puts a dedicated Entropy field at the start of the PDS header. That keeps PDS and SES inside the 128-byte switch ASIC parsing window.

10. What is the rule governing UET CRC and the Transport Security Sublayer (TSS) on a UET packet?

The UEC Specification v1.0 lets a packet use either the UET CRC or TSS, but never both at once. TSS also does not work with UUD. Keep that rule in mind when you configure a link.

11. Contrast the four UEC packet delivery modes (ROD, RUD, RUDI, UUD) and their primary AI/HPC workloads.
  • ROD: Delivers in order, using the receiver NIC reorder buffer. It suits HPC, MPI, and serialized control traffic.
  • RUD: Places data out of order with Direct Data Placement and selective retry. It suits AI model parallelism and bulk transfers.
  • RUDI: Delivers unordered, and it is safe to retry or write several times. It suits RMA writes and AI gradient updates.
  • UUD: Best-effort, with no PSNs, no ACKs or NACKs, and no TSS. It suits telemetry and inference logs.
12. How do NSCC and RCCC differ in UEC end-to-end congestion management, and what is the exact RCCC credit unit size?

NSCC is sender-driven. It adjusts the congestion window (cwnd) and the packet-spraying entropy, based on RTT, the ECN flags in NACKs, and ACK_CC telemetry. RCCC is target-driven. Senders request credit through Credit Target (req_cc_state), and the receiver grants credit from its local Credit Pool through a 24-bit ACK field. Each credit is 256 bytes. You can think of a credit as a fixed unit of permission to send.

13. How does UEC link-level CBFC improve upon both IEEE PFC and InfiniBand CBFC?

Credits are the reason the link can run lossless without pause storms. In practice, UEC CBFC raises the capacity from 8 PFC priorities and 16 InfiniBand VLs to 32 Virtual Channels per port. Its capability is negotiated over LLDP. It tracks Consumed and Freed credits per VC, and it sends credit updates out of band, in the physical sublayer, as PCS CtlOS CP_Update messages at line rate.

14. How do UEC DSCP Packet Trimming and Link Layer Reliability (LLR) accelerate loss recovery?

With DSCP Packet Trimming, you see a congested switch cut the data payload off trim-eligible packets. It forwards the header on a high-priority queue to the target FEP. The target then sends a targeted fast-retransmission request, a NACK, and it never writes the trimmed header into GPU memory. With LLR, neighboring link partners give each frame a SEQ number in the MAC preamble and keep a copy in an ASIC replay buffer. They retransmit the frame locally when a timeout or a NACK SEQ shows it was lost.

15. What are the components of UEC In-Network Collectives (INC), and how do they interact with xCCLs?

You will find three parts in UEC INC. Put simply, the switches do the adding, and the hosts send only one copy. There are INC-capable switches (sFEPs) that run switch INC agents, an out-of-band INC Manager that talks over gRPC, and the Libfabric fi_collective API. User-space xCCLs coordinate GPU collectives on the hosts. INC instead elects a root-of-tree spine switch, which aggregates and distributes the collective vectors inside the switch silicon.

16. Why do scale-up pods replace PCIe with NVLink, SUE-T, or UALink, and what is the physical scale limit of a scale-up pod?

Read this answer as a case for scale-up fabrics. PCIe 5.0 and PCIe 6.0 cannot match the 100 to 300 ns latency and the multi-Tbps per-xPU bandwidth that tightly coupled tensor parallelism and MoE all-to-all routing need. For you as a designer, cables shorter than 4 meters and power limits keep a scale-up pod to 1 to 4 racks and up to 1,000 xPUs.

17. In OCP SUE-T (ESUN), what is the difference between the 6-byte and 12-byte AFH Gen 2 headers, and what fields reside in the Reliability Header (RH)?

When you read a trace, the header tells you which format you are looking at. If your pod is single-hop, the 6-byte compressed AFH Gen 2 header serves it. It encodes the Destination xPU, Traffic Class, and Source xPU inside the DMAC and SMAC positions. The 12-byte non-compressed header serves two-hop topologies. The RH carries VER, OP, a 10-bit xPU ID, a 16-bit NPSN, VC, a Partition field for tenant isolation, and a 16-bit APSN.

18. How do the FIFO and ARM AXI 4.0 on-die interfaces differ in SUE-T accelerators?

Picture the two interfaces side by side. The FIFO interface streams raw PDUs, using a 10-bit xPU ID and a 2-bit VC ID, and it has no memory-transaction semantics. The ARM AXI 4.0 interface exposes five independent transaction channels on two virtual channels. Its AR, AW, and W requests travel on VC0, and its B and R responses travel on VC1.

19. Explain the UALink 1.0 station/lane hierarchy (6.4 Tbps) and how 64B TL flits pack into 640B DL flits and RS(544, 514) PCS codewords.

Work the arithmetic from the bottom up. If you build a UALink 1.0 xPU, it has up to 8 stations of 4 lanes of 200 Gbps SerDes, which makes 32 lanes and 6.4 Tbps. The Transaction Layer encodes commands and data into 64-byte TL flits. The Data Link layer packs 10 TL flits into one 640-byte DL flit, and adding 40 bytes of FEC makes a 680-byte wire unit. That unit aligns 1:1 into a single RS(544, 514) PCS codeword, which travels with 1-way or 2-way interleaving.

20. Trace the UALink 1.0 GVA -> NPA -> SPA address translation pipeline and explain why UALink uses software PGAS coherence instead of hardware snoops.

You can trace it this way: a compute unit issues a request in the Guest Virtual Address space. The source MMU and the Address Handle translate that GVA into a globally unique Network Physical Address, which carries a 10-bit DSTACCID and SRCACCID. UALink Switches route the DL flit using the 10-bit ACC-ID. The destination Link MMU then translates the NPA into a local System Physical Address in HBM. UALink 1.0 uses software-managed PGAS coherence (RMA_FENCE, RMA_SYNC, NOTIFY) instead of hardware directory snoops. That lets coherence scale across 1,000 multi-vendor accelerators.
