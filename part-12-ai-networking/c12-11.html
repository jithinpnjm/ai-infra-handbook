Why this matters for AI infrastructure​

Picture a training job that runs well for hours, then slows to a crawl. The cause is often a single congested leaf uplink, not the GPUs. An AI Ethernet switch is not a passive box of fast ports. It is the place where hundreds of RDMA Queue Pairs meet and compete for the same buffer memory. Every microsecond, frames enter lossless or lossy queues, and microbursts get absorbed in shared silicon memory. A microburst is a very short spike of traffic, often lasting only microseconds, that arrives faster than a port can send it on. On a large multi-rack cluster, every optical link can report UP with zero Forward Error Correction uncorrectable errors. Yet a few destination-facing leaf uplinks can still suffer hash collisions or incast congestion. That local contention stalls an entire synchronous training job, because every other GPU in the job is waiting at the same barrier.

Traditional enterprise Ethernet switches and standard five-tuple Equal-Cost Multi-Path routing were built for thousands of short, high-entropy TCP flows. The five-tuple is the set of source address, destination address, source port, destination port and protocol that identifies one flow. Equal-Cost Multi-Path, or ECMP, spreads flows across several equal links by hashing that five-tuple. High entropy means many different five-tuples, so the hashes spread out nicely. They were never designed for a handful of synchronized RoCEv2 elephant flows generated by NCCL collectives. An elephant flow is one very large stream of data, and a few of them can overwhelm a path. Under static five-tuple hashing, two fat elephant flows often land on the same spine uplink while sibling links sit half empty. Think of two heavy trucks choosing the same lane of a five-lane highway while the other four lanes stay empty. That collision caps effective cluster bisection bandwidth around sixty percent of physical wire capacity. The buffer adds a second problem. On a sliced buffer design, a port hit by an AI microburst can draw only from its small dedicated slice. That port drops packets even while the rest of the switch memory sits idle.

NVIDIA Spectrum-X addresses these limits by designing the switching silicon, the server-edge network adapter, and the network operating system together. Designing the three together matters, because a clever switch paired with a dumb adapter still loses packets at the edge. Four pieces do most of the work.

  1. The NVIDIA Spectrum-4 switch ASIC in the SN5600 and SN5600D gives 51.2 Tbps of switching capacity over one monolithic, fully shared buffer. Its hardware microsecond fairness and What Just Happened drop-reason telemetry show you why packets were dropped. The SN2201 out-of-band management switch sits alongside it, on a separate network, so you can still reach a broken rack when the main fabric is the problem.
  2. Spectrum-X pairs Spectrum-4 switches with BlueField-3 SuperNICs, BlueField-3 DPUs, or ConnectX-7 and ConnectX-8 adapters. You manage the whole stack declaratively through Cumulus Linux NVUE, DOCA, NetQ, and NVIDIA Base Command Manager. Declarative means you describe the state you want, and the system works out the steps to reach it.
  3. Adaptive Routing and Selective Dynamic Load Balancing watch real-time port utilization, queue depth, and RoCEv2 Base Transport Header RDMA Write opcodes. The Base Transport Header is the fixed header in each RoCEv2 packet, and its opcode field says what kind of RDMA operation the packet carries. The switch then sprays individual packets across all equal-cost spine links. Effective AI fabric bandwidth rises from roughly 60 percent to 95 percent.
  4. BlueField-3 and ConnectX-7 or ConnectX-8 hardware place out-of-order packets directly into GPU memory, without Go-Back-N retransmission storms. Go-Back-N is a retransmission style that resends everything from the first lost packet onward, so one drop can trigger a large resend. Meanwhile, Spectrum-4 hardware telemetry paces sender injection rates within a single round-trip time.

1. Spectrum-4 switch architecture, monolithic shared buffers, and SuperPOD topologies​

Suppose you are choosing switches for a new AI cluster. The datasheet quotes a port speed, and it is tempting to stop there. But port speed alone does not guarantee collective throughput. If a switch has low port radix, the fabric needs extra spine tiers, and each tier adds hop latency and optics cost. Worse, if the switch splits its packet buffer into rigid per-port slices, synchronized GPU microbursts overflow individual slices. That overflow causes packet drops or pause storms, even while most of the switch memory sits unused. A pause storm is a cascade where one congested port asks its neighbors to stop sending, and they ask theirs, until a large part of the fabric is frozen.

1.1 Spectrum-4 51.2 Tbps ASIC, port radix, breakout modes, and SN2201 OOB switches​

In an AI Ethernet fabric, headline switching capacity only helps as far as the radix and buffer architecture let you use it. Radix is the number of independent ports you can fan out to servers and spines without adding another switching tier. Think of radix as the number of doors in a building. A building with many doors lets people leave without climbing through extra floors. The NVIDIA Spectrum-4 switch ASIC delivers 51.2 Tbps of bidirectional switching capacity. A single monolithic chip reaches that figure with 512 lanes of 100G PAM4 SerDes. Monolithic here means one chip rather than several chips stitched together, and that matters for the buffer story in the next section.

Each physical port on an SN5600 or SN5600D uses eight lanes of PAM4 SerDes. That is why one switch can run as 64 ports at 800GbE. With optical or copper splitter cables, the same switch breaks out into 128 ports at 400GbE, 256 ports at 200GbE, or 512 ports at 100GbE. A splitter cable works like a power strip. One physical port becomes several smaller ports, each with fewer lanes. High radix lets you build wide two-tier leaf-spine Clos fabrics. A Clos fabric is a layout of leaf switches that connect to the servers and spine switches that connect the leaves. You do not need a third, super-spine tier until you deploy thousands of GPUs, because two tiers already reach a large number of endpoints when each switch is wide.

The breakout math shows that the total stays fixed at 51.2 Tbps. Only the number of ports changes as you split them. Choose the breakout that matches the number of servers you need to attach. A breakout that gives each server one port is simple to cable and simple to debug. A breakout that splits one server across several ports can be useful, but then every port needs its own rail and its own health check. Pick the simpler layout unless you have a clear reason to pay for the complexity. Ask the question the other way around as well. Count your servers and their adapters first, then pick the breakout that gives each adapter one port. Doing it in that order keeps the switch from being the bottleneck.

1.2 Monolithic fully shared buffer versus sliced port-allocated buffers​

Picture a synchronized collective phase, or a storage checkpoint write. Dozens of high-speed ports burst at the same moment toward one destination port. That is a many-to-one incast. The switch must absorb the rate mismatch in on-chip SRAM packet memory while congestion signaling takes effect. SRAM is the fast memory on the chip that holds packets while they wait for their turn on the wire. Congestion signaling is the message the switch sends back to the senders, asking them to slow down.

In a standard multi-core or sliced switch, the total buffer is split into fixed slices, one per port. Think of a parking garage where every car owner gets one reserved bay, even when the other bays are empty. Take an eight-megabyte microburst that hits Port 1 on a switch with a one-megabyte cap per port. Port 1 fills its slice at once and drops seven megabytes. Those drops happen even though the memory on the other ports sits idle. Spectrum-4 pools all internal packet memory into a monolithic fully shared buffer. Any congested port can borrow up to a shared threshold, so it absorbs the same transient AI microburst with zero drops. The same garage now lets any car take any empty bay, up to a limit that keeps one car from taking the whole building.

The diagram shows the two designs side by side. On the sliced side, the idle memory cannot help the hot port. On the Spectrum side, the hot port borrows from the shared pool. Read each box from top to bottom, and you can follow the burst from the moment it arrives to the moment it leaves. The dotted arrow on the sliced side is the key detail, because it shows the idle slices are blocked from helping.

Notice the middle column. The sliced design drops packets, or triggers early pauses, while memory sits unused. The shared pool does neither for the same burst. Use this difference to judge any switch you evaluate. Ask the vendor how the buffer is allocated, and ask what happens to one hot port under a burst. A vague answer is a warning sign. Ask them to show the burst test in writing, with the port count, the buffer size and the cap they used. A good vendor can answer those questions on the spot, and a weak one will change the subject to port speed.

The third row is easy to skip, and it matters just as much. Hardware fairness keeps a single large elephant flow from pushing out the small control and barrier frames that the collective needs to keep moving. If the barrier frames are late, every GPU in the job waits.

1.3 Spectrum-4 and SN2201 in the DGX GB300 SuperPOD architecture​

Now see how these pieces fit together in the NVIDIA DGX GB300 SuperPOD reference architecture. Read this section as a floor plan. Each piece has a place, and the place tells you why it is there. Each rack is a liquid-cooled DGX GB300 NVL72 system with eighteen compute trays. Eight racks form one Scalable Unit. Every compute tray has two network adapter families on its front cold-aisle panel. Four ConnectX-8 NICs serve the rail-aligned compute fabric. One dual-port BlueField-3 BF3240 DPU connects to a two-layer Spectrum-4 SN5600 and SN5600D Ethernet fabric.

The design avoids one tempting shortcut. It does not bond the two BlueField-3 ports into a shared interface, where unpredictable TCP bursts could collide with storage traffic. Instead, the SuperPOD isolates the two ports on separate fabrics.

  1. Port 0 on each BlueField-3 BF3240 carries only the lossless RoCEv2 High-Performance Storage network.
  2. Port 1 carries the In-Band Management and NFS user storage network.
  3. Compute leaves connect to the SN5600D spine tier with a deliberate three-to-one blocking ratio, sized to per-tray storage demand. Storage leaves connect nonblocking, so storage arrays never bottleneck at the leaf-to-spine cut.
  4. Two management leaves serve Base Command Manager, Slurm, Run:ai, container registries, and NFS storage. Two OOB aggregation spines gather the SN2201 management switches into a dedicated VXLAN domain.

A common mistake is to size storage from the GPU count and then discover the checkpoint writes take far longer than the training step they are meant to protect. 1.4 Evaluating switch capacity at the bottleneck cut in normal and N-1 degraded states​

Vendor datasheets highlight total chip throughput. A distributed job, though, only feels the narrowest bisection cut on its traffic path. The bisection cut is the line you draw through the fabric to split it into two halves, and the capacity across that line is the bottleneck for half-to-half traffic. So when you review any Spectrum leaf-spine topology, separate five metrics that people often mix up. They are single-port speed, port radix, aggregate switch capacity, shared-buffer behavior, and usable bisection capacity at the active traffic cut. N-1 means the design still works after one component fails, and a degraded state is the design running at N-1.

$$ \text{Effective Available Capacity} = \text{Capacity of the relevant path cut after topology, policy, and } N-1 \text{ failure constraints} $$

Read the formula this way. Your real cluster bandwidth is capped by the surviving uplinks across the leaf-to-spine cut. Include maintenance drains and single-link failures when you work out that limit. The word "relevant" in the formula matters. You only count the paths that the traffic can actually use, after policy has removed any that are closed.

Here is the arithmetic for one rack of 16 GPU nodes. Each node sends traffic off the rack over two 400 Gbps RoCEv2 rails, which is 800 Gbps per node. A rail is one dedicated network path from a GPU's adapter into the fabric, so two rails per node means two separate paths. Multiply the per-node figure by the node count, and you get the demand the rack's uplinks must absorb. The steps below follow that order.

Worked Leaf-to-Spine Cut Capacity in Normal and N-1 Degraded States (V9 Ch. 2 & Ch. 10):
  - Rack Downlink Demand:       16 GPU nodes x (2 x 400 Gbps RoCEv2 rails = 800 Gbps/node) = 12,800 Gbps worst-case off-rack injection
  - Normal Uplink Capacity:     8 active uplinks x 400 Gbps = 3,200 Gbps -> 12,800 / 3,200 = 4.0:1 (4:1) normal oversubscription ratio
  - Degraded N-1 Uplink Cut:    1 uplink drained/failed = 7 active uplinks x 400 Gbps = 2,800 Gbps -> 12,800 / 2,800 = 4.57:1 oversubscription
  - Spine Drain Scenario:       Draining 1 of 2 spines shrinks nexthop-group 40 from 2 active members to 1, concentrating cross-rack traffic
                                onto half the uplinks and driving tx_ecn_marked_prio3 up 5.87x (from 15,000 to 88,120 while rx_pfc_prio3 stays 0)

One failed uplink moves the oversubscription from 4:1 to 4.57:1 with no change in demand. A drained spine goes further. It pushes ECN marks on priority 3 up 5.87 times, even though no PFC pauses arrive. ECN marks are the warning flags a switch sets on packets when a queue starts to fill, and priority 3 is the lossless class that RoCEv2 uses. Notice the phrase "no PFC pauses arrive." The marks rise first, and the pauses would come later if the queue kept growing. Plan for that degraded state before you schedule maintenance.

Always size and validate leaf-to-spine cuts under both normal and degraded spine conditions. Then planned switch maintenance never triggers a cluster-wide training stall. The check is quick on paper, and it can prevent the kind of incident that wakes the whole team.

2. End-to-end Spectrum-X platform: Spectrum-4, BlueField-3, ConnectX, and DOCA​

A switch cannot fix a bad endpoint on its own. Picture a leaf port that reports UP at full speed with zero physical errors. The GPU server behind it still cannot be reached, because an embedded DPU service failed during boot. A downgraded host PCIe slot can cap RDMA throughput at a fraction of line rate, and the switch counters still look clean. You need to check the endpoint as well as the switch. A good rule is to check each side of a link before you blame the link itself. The switch and the adapter are two halves of one conversation. If only one half is listening, the link looks dead from both ends, and the cable is the last thing to suspect. Section 2 covers how to check both halves in a fixed order, starting with the card's operating mode and ending with the host's view of its own rails.

2.1 BlueField-3 operating modes and three-layer fault isolation​

Spectrum-X works end to end. The Spectrum-4 switch and the BlueField-3 or ConnectX endpoint cooperate in hardware. On the server side, NVIDIA ships BlueField-3 in two hardware families, and their default modes are easy to confuse. The mode decides who owns the network path inside the card, so a wrong guess sends you to the wrong log.

  1. BlueField-3 DPU SKUs, such as the dual-port BF3240, default to DPU mode. Also called Embedded CPU Function Ownership, this mode puts the embedded Arm subsystem, running DOCA Linux, in charge of the NIC resources and the embedded switch. The Arm subsystem is a small processor inside the card, and DOCA is NVIDIA's software framework that runs on it.
  2. In DPU mode, the first packet of a flow crosses an Arm representor slow path. There the Arm control plane checks policy and programs a hardware flow rule. A representor is the software handle that stands in for a hardware port. Later packets then take the hardware fast path directly. This is why the first packet of a new connection can look slower than the rest.
  3. BlueField-3 SuperNIC SKUs, such as the BF3140, default to NIC mode and act as a dedicated Spectrum-X RoCEv2 endpoint. The host driver manages Queue Pairs directly. Hardware engines handle Adaptive Routing packet reordering and telemetry-driven congestion control, with no Arm virtual-switch dependency. In plain words, the card stays out of the way and lets the host talk straight to the fabric.

The diagram gives you a path to follow. Start at the switch uplink, check the Arm policy service next, and check the host netdev last. Each layer can fail on its own. The order matters because each check answers a question the next one depends on. If the uplink is down, the other two checks tell you nothing. If the Arm service failed, the host netdev will look dead even though the cable is fine.

When operating BlueField DPUs in DPU mode, treat the host, the DPU Arm subsystem, and the Spectrum switch as three separate failure domains. A failure domain is a group of parts that can fail together, so keeping them separate means one failure does not hide another. Gate node scheduler readiness on all three layers, not on switch link state alone. Prefer a supported, packaged DOCA service over a custom DOCA application, unless a dedicated engineering team owns long-term maintenance. Never deploy a DOCA SDK sample directly as production infrastructure. A sample is written to show one API call, and it has no error handling, upgrade path or monitoring.

2.2 ConnectX and BlueField-3 local I/O paths: PCIe downgrades, NUMA locality, and multi-rail selection​

On the host side of a ConnectX-7, ConnectX-8, or BlueField-3 SuperNIC, a link delivers line rate only when three things cooperate. The local PCIe root complex, the NUMA topology, and the collective communication library must all drive it properly. The PCIe root complex is the piece of the CPU that talks to the slots, and NUMA decides which memory and cores sit closest to each slot. The collective communication library, NCCL in this chapter, decides which adapter each GPU rank uses. If any one of the three points the wrong way, the link runs slower than its label.

  1. Compare LnkCap against LnkSta in lspci -vv to check the negotiated PCIe speed and width. A 200GbE adapter needs 25.0 GB/s of PCIe bandwidth. If it drops from PCIe Gen5 x16 to Gen4 x8, it bottlenecks at 16.0 GB/s.
  2. Run nvidia-smi topo -m to confirm that each GPU pairs with a NIC on the same PCIe switch hierarchy (PIX) on its local NUMA node. A pair that crosses the CPU socket interconnect (SYS) is a sign of misplacement.
  3. Capture sixty-second ethtool -S byte-counter deltas across all rails during a collective run. That check confirms that each local GPU rank binds to its own dedicated mlx5 RDMA device, rather than collapsing onto a single rail.

Avoid generic host LACP bonding on AI RoCEv2 rails, because bonding hides the physical rail topology from NCCL. The second is the rail count, which tells you whether the GPUs are actually using the adapters they have. A slow adapter with a healthy rail split is one problem. A fast adapter with every rank crowded onto one rail is another. Fixing the wrong one wastes a maintenance window, so read both numbers before you change anything.

3. Spectrum-X Adaptive Routing and selective per-packet RoCEv2 spraying​

Think about a conventional enterprise data center. Thousands of independent TCP connections create enough randomness in their five-tuple headers to spread traffic evenly across spine switches. An AI collective works differently. Each GPU opens only a few Queue Pairs, and those blast RoCEv2 elephant flows at the same moment. When two elephant flows hash onto the same uplink, that link saturates at one hundred percent. Parallel spine links sit underused, and cluster-wide bisection efficiency falls to roughly sixty percent. The contrast is the whole story of this section. A large pool of small random flows balances itself, and a small pool of big synchronized flows does not. The rest of the section shows how the switch recovers the balance that the traffic no longer gives it for free. Read each subsection as one more tool in that recovery, and keep the grocery-lane picture in mind as the reason the tools are needed.

3.1 Why traditional 5-tuple Static Load Balancing caps AI Ethernet fabrics at 60 percent​

In a standard IP leaf-spine Clos fabric, a leaf switch learns equal-cost paths to a remote GPU across all spine switches through BGP Equal-Cost Multi-Path routing. BGP is the routing protocol that tells each leaf which spines can reach a destination. With Static Load Balancing, the switch Packet Forwarding Engine computes a deterministic CRC32 hash over each packet's five-tuple. A deterministic hash gives the same answer every time for the same input, so a given flow always lands on the same link. The five fields are source IP, destination IP, IP protocol, source UDP port, and the fixed RoCEv2 destination UDP port.

To keep one link flap from rehashing every active flow, modern ASICs map the hash into a fixed resilient hashing indirection table. An indirection table is a lookup list of buckets, and each bucket points to one link. Only the buckets assigned to a failed link move when a port goes down. However, neither modulo arithmetic nor resilient hashing looks at real-time link bandwidth or queue depth. Modulo arithmetic is the simple remainder trick that picks a link by dividing the hash by the number of links. So when two large flows collide on one spine uplink, while two smaller flows land on a second uplink, the first uplink saturates and the second sits half-empty. The hash does its job, but it does not know how busy each link is.

A useful way to hold this in your head is to picture a grocery store with eight checkout lanes and a rule that every shopper must pick a lane by the color of their shirt. Shirt color is fixed for the whole trip, so a family that all wears red lines up at one lane while the others stand empty. The store has plenty of lanes, and the rule still piles people into one of them. Static hashing behaves the same way. The number of spine links is not the problem. The rule that assigns flows to them is.

4. BlueField-3 hardware out-of-order Direct Data Placement and telemetry-driven congestion control​

Spraying the packets of one Queue Pair across several spines means those packets take paths with slightly different queue depths and fiber lengths. They therefore reach the destination out of Packet Sequence Number order. The Packet Sequence Number, or PSN, is a counter on each packet that tells the receiver where it belongs in the stream. On a standard RDMA NIC, one out-of-sequence packet is enough to trigger a sequence-gap Negative Acknowledgment. That NAK starts a Go-Back-N retransmission storm, and throughput collapses. A storm here means the sender resends a long run of packets because one gap was seen, and the resent packets add load to the same congested links.

4.1 Why per-packet spraying breaks standard RDMA NICs and how hardware DDP solves it​

BlueField-3 SuperNICs and ConnectX-7 or ConnectX-8 adapters hide per-packet spraying from applications. They do it with hardware out-of-order Direct Data Placement. Direct Data Placement means the adapter writes each payload straight to its final address in memory, so it does not need the packets to arrive in order to assemble the message. Here is how the receiver handles one RDMA Write burst.

  1. The First or Only packet carries the RDMA Extended Transport Header. That header holds the base virtual memory address, the Remote Key, and the total DMA length. Each later Middle or Last packet carries its Packet Sequence Number in the Base Transport Header.
  2. The Path MTU and starting PSN live in the hardware Queue Pair context. So the receiving BlueField-3 SuperNIC computes each packet's destination offset with arithmetic. It then DMAs the payload straight into GPU High-Bandwidth Memory, without staging out-of-order packets in NIC SRAM.
  3. The SuperNIC tracks arrivals in a sliding hardware PSN bitmap. It holds back the final acknowledgment and Completion Queue Element until every delayed packet has arrived and the bitmap is contiguous.

$$ \text{Target Address}(\text{PSN}) = \text{VA}{\text{base}} + (\text{PSN} - \text{PSN}{\text{start}}) \times \text{MTU} $$

This equation tells the receiving NIC the exact destination byte address in GPU memory for any packet. It needs only the packet's Packet Sequence Number and the Queue Pair MTU.

Read the sequence from top to bottom, and you will see the packets land out of order. Packets 202 and 203 arrive before 201, yet each one lands at its own address with zero NAKs. The trick is that the address comes from the packet number, not from the arrival order. Packet 203 knows it belongs three slots after the base, so it can be written there right away, even before its neighbor shows up. Think of mailbox slots numbered in advance. A letter can be dropped into slot three without waiting for slots one and two to fill.

4.2 Spectrum-X hardware telemetry-driven congestion control​

Even with perfect per-packet spraying across the spine tier, congestion can still form at the last hop. That is the port from the destination leaf to the server, labeled CP4. It appears when several GPUs write to one receiver at once, as in an incast or a parallel storage checkpoint. Classic DCQCN relies on probabilistic ECN marks. DCQCN is the congestion control scheme that reacts to those marks, and it is the baseline this section compares against. The marks are binary, which means a packet either carries one or it does not. They tell the sender NIC only that congestion exists. They do not say how full the queue is or how many flows compete. As a result, a classic sender cuts its rate blindly, then probes slowly upward, which produces a sawtooth in bandwidth. A sawtooth is the up-and-down pattern you see on a graph when a sender repeatedly overshoots and then creeps back.

In Spectrum-X Congestion Control, the Spectrum-4 switch ASIC and the BlueField-3 SuperNIC replace blind binary probing with hardware telemetry-driven rate control. When a Spectrum-4 egress queue crosses its target threshold, the switch injects exact telemetry into congestion feedback packets. That telemetry covers instantaneous queue depth, queue drain rate, and active link utilization. Competing BlueField-3 senders use it to set their exact fair-share drain rate within a single round-trip time. The queue drains smoothly, without wasting the wire or triggering PFC pause storms. The big change is the content of the message. The sender now knows how full the queue is, not just that it is full. Think of a highway with a sensor that tells each car exactly how many cars are ahead and how fast the lane is clearing. A driver who gets that message eases off by the right amount, instead of slamming the brakes and then flooring the gas. Smooth pacing is what keeps the queue short without leaving the wire idle.

Notice what the telemetry does not do. It does not reroute the flow, and it does not change the packet order. It only tells the senders how fast they may push right now. The routing and reordering jobs belong to the earlier sections, so each feature has one clear job.

Worked Spectrum-X Telemetry-Driven Congestion Control vs Blind Binary Cut (code/part12/spectrum_x_switches.py):
  - Switch Egress Port & Queue:  400.0 Gbps port, 2 active flows; queue occupancy = 160.0 KB (target = 64.0 KB, max = 256.0 KB, excess = 96 KB)
  - Telemetry Occupancy Ratio:   (160.0 - 64.0) / (256.0 - 64.0) = 96.0 / 192.0 = 0.5

When you read the last two lines of the worked example, the contrast is clear. A blind cut takes a 400 Gbps flow down to 200 Gbps and then recovers slowly. Telemetry sets 185 Gbps per flow within one round-trip time. The round-trip time is the delay for a packet to reach the receiver and for the reply to return, and a sender that reacts within one of those delays can correct course before the queue grows much further.

Never enable per-packet fabric spraying unless the receiving RDMA adapters support hardware out-of-order Direct Data Placement. The rule repeats the warning from section 3 on purpose, because a fabric that sprays into an adapter without that support fails in a way that looks like a slow network rather than a broken one.

5. Spectrum telemetry, What Just Happened, and the 4-branch fault decision tree​

When a distributed training run slows down on an Ethernet fabric, do not treat the whole switch network as one suspect. That wastes critical triage time. Combine host ethtool -S deltas with Spectrum switch queue counters, ECN marks, PFC pause frames, and What Just Happened hardware drop-reason telemetry. What Just Happened, or WJH, is the Spectrum feature that records why a packet was dropped, not just that it was. Joining those signals routes any incident into one of four root-cause branches. Think of the four branches as four doors. The evidence tells you which door to open, and you do not need to open all of them.

5.1 Separating physical faults, healthy congestion control, hot cuts, and ECMP imbalance​

The four-branch Spectrum telemetry decision tree separates physical corruption from normal congestion pacing, oversubscribed cuts, and routing or rail imbalance. Each branch names a different owner of the fix. A physical fault belongs to whoever handles cables and optics. Healthy congestion control needs no fix at all, and that is the branch people most often misread as a fault.

Start at the top of the tree and let the physical counters decide first. Only when the links are clean do the queue and pause signals matter. A physical fault makes every other counter unreliable, because retransmissions inflate the ECN and PFC numbers. So the first question is always whether the cable and optic are sound. If they are, the second question is whether the queues are full because the design is pacing senders correctly, or because one place in the fabric is too small. The last question is whether the spread of traffic across links is even. Asking the questions in that order keeps each answer clean enough to trust.

6. Cumulus Linux, declarative NVUE, NetQ, and Base Command Manager operations​

Suppose you edit configuration files by hand on dozens of leaf and spine switches. Sooner or later, the configs drift apart. One port that reverts from DSCP trust to PCP trust will silently drop RoCEv2 traffic into a lossy queue. DSCP and PCP are two ways of marking a packet's priority, and a switch that trusts the wrong one will put RoCEv2 traffic in the wrong queue. Modern AI fabrics avoid hand edits by managing every switch through one declarative object model. Instead of typing each change, you describe the state you want, and the system makes every switch match it.

6.1 Declarative switch management with NVUE and BCM cmsh​

NVIDIA Spectrum switches run Cumulus Linux, a native Debian-based Network Operating System. Debian-based means it is built on the Debian Linux family. It exposes NVIDIA User Experience, called NVUE, as its unified declarative management model. You and your automation pipelines reach the nvued daemon through three equivalent interfaces. A daemon is a background service that waits for requests, and here it is the service that applies your configuration.

  2. The NVUE REST API on HTTPS port 8765 exposes the same object tree for configuration-as-code automation.

As you read the code block, keep the three nvconfigurationmode options in mind. Auto mode generates indexed rules for you. Manual mode ignores those generated rules. File mode, which DGX SuperPODs use, points to startup.yaml. The choice decides who owns the configuration. In auto mode, the cluster manager writes the lines. In file mode, your versioned file is the single source, and the manager reads it back.

A transit MAC is the hardware address of a device that passes traffic through a switch without belonging to it, such as the far end of a spine link. If the cluster manager treats that address as a server, it may record the wrong port for a node. Declaring the inter-switch ports as uplinks tells the manager to ignore those addresses. That is the reason the uplink step is easy to forget, and the reason it appears in the cmsh block above.

Before you move on to the Spectrum telemetry, pause and check the configuration source. A switch that was configured by hand three months ago and a switch built from the versioned file can look identical on the day they are compared, and then drift apart after the next change. The file is what keeps them the same.

Always manage Spectrum switches from a single versioned startup.yaml or BCM NVUE source of truth. Register every inter-switch link as an uplink in cmsh, so that you never let transit MACs corrupt port discovery. A single source of truth also gives you one place to look when two switches disagree.

Common first mistakes​

Each mistake below looks reasonable on a busy night, which is why it keeps coming back. Read them as a short list of reflexes to unlearn. Most of them share one root, which is trusting a single green signal instead of the full set of counters.

  1. Treating switch aggregate bandwidth as usable collective bisection bandwidth. Check whether you see static five-tuple ECMP collisions or an oversubscribed leaf-to-spine cut clamp cross-rack traffic. The datasheet number is the ceiling for a perfect workload, not the number your job will see.
  2. Enabling per-packet spraying toward RDMA endpoints that lack BlueField-3 or ConnectX hardware out-of-order Direct Data Placement. Without hardware DDP, out-of-order arrival triggers sequence-gap NAKs and Go-Back-N retransmission storms. The fabric looks busy, but the job makes little progress.
  3. Assuming BlueField DPU mode defaults apply to BlueField SuperNIC SKUs as well. An up switch port never proves host network reachability when the DPU Arm policy service failed during boot. Check the SKU before you check anything else.
  4. Resetting shared switch or NIC counters during triage instead of capturing time-windowed deltas. Clearing counters erases WJH tail-drop history and the multi-leaf rx_pfc_prio3 gradient. Capture the numbers first, then compare two snapshots.
  5. Certifying a new rack or switch OS upgrade on ping, link-up status, or one isolated pairwise ib_write_bw test. Single-flow tests never show you multi-rank AllReduce contention. A rack that passes alone can still stall when twenty GPUs push at once.

Interview angles​

Interviewers ask these questions to hear how you move from a symptom to a layer. Each answer below starts with the evidence, then names the layer it points to, and then gives the fix. Practice saying the answer that way, and the structure will come easily under pressure. Say the layer out loud before you name a command. That habit shows the interviewer you know where the evidence lives, and it keeps you from listing every counter you have ever seen.

  1. "Why does standard Ethernet with 5-tuple ECMP struggle to exceed 60 percent effective bandwidth on AI training collectives, and how does NVIDIA Spectrum-X push that to 95 percent?"
    • Strong answer: Standard five-tuple Static Load Balancing hashes source and destination IPs, UDP ports, and protocol through CRC32. It never looks at real-time link load or queue depth. GPU collectives produce only a few synchronized elephant flows with little header variety, so several fat flows collide onto the same spine uplink at full utilization. Meanwhile, sibling links sit half-empty, which caps effective cluster bisection bandwidth near sixty percent. Spectrum-X fixes this with an end-to-end hardware design in three parts. Spectrum-4 switches use Adaptive Routing and Selective Dynamic Load Balancing on RoCEv2 RDMA Write opcodes to spray packets across all equal-cost spine links. BlueField-3 SuperNICs and ConnectX-7 or ConnectX-8 adapters place out-of-order packets directly into GPU memory, without Go-Back-N retransmission storms. Hardware telemetry-driven congestion control then paces each sender to its exact fair-share rate within one round-trip time. When you explain this in an interview, start with the hash, because that is where the collisions begin.
  2. "A 256-GPU cluster passes pairwise ib_write_bw at 392 Gbps, every Spectrum switch port is UP with zero FEC uncorrectable errors, yet one rack is a persistent straggler under two concurrent training jobs. How do you isolate the root cause?"
    • Strong answer: Pairwise ib_write_bw tests a single point-to-point flow. It never creates concurrent multi-rank convergence on shared leaf-to-spine cuts. Because the physical FEC and CRC counters are zero, you can rule out bad optics. Instead, compare queue-level telemetry on the slow rack's leaf with a healthy rack's leaf during the same collective window. Run show what-just-happened drop-reason all to look for Tail drop (queue full) events. Compare ethtool -S deltas for rx_ecn_marked_prio3 and rx_pfc_prio3 across leaves. Check that no switch port on the slow rack's path drifted to trust pcp instead of trust dscp. Finally, confirm balanced rx_bytes_phy deltas across all host NIC rails.
  3. "After a maintenance reboot, a Spectrum leaf switch shows the server uplink swp14 up at full speed with zero errors, yet the GPU host cannot reach any fabric peers. What happened?"
    • Strong answer: When a server uses a BlueField DPU in its default DPU mode, you can observe three layers separately. Layer 3 is the wire between the DPU and the Spectrum leaf switch. Layer 2 is inside the BlueField Arm control plane and embedded switch. Layer 1 is the host network interface. Seeing swp14 up on the leaf only proves Layer 3. Over Out-of-Band management, the BlueField Arm OS shows that openvswitch-policy-load.service failed during boot. As a result, the embedded switch never received the fast-path flow rules needed to bring enp1s0f0np0 up. Recovery means fixing the DPU policy or image over OOB management, then gating node readiness on all three layers.

2. How does Spectrum-4's monolithic fully shared buffer differ from a sliced switch buffer during an 8.0 MB AI microburst on a 64-port switch with 64.0 MB total buffer memory?

Picture Port 1 hit by that burst on a sliced switch. The switch divides its memory into fixed per-port slices, so Port 1 hits its cap and drops seven megabytes. The rest of the memory sits idle the whole time. Spectrum-4 lets Port 1 borrow up to the shared threshold. It absorbs the whole 8.0 MB microburst with zero bytes dropped, and it enforces hardware microsecond fairness.

  - Sliced Buffer:              64.0 MB / 64 = 1.0 MB per-port cap -> 7.0 MB dropped (63.0 MB locked idle on Ports 2..64)
  - Monolithic Shared Buffer:   Dynamic alpha = 25% -> 16.0 MB cap -> borrows 8.0 MB cleanly with 0.0 MB dropped

7. How does the default operating mode of a BlueField-3 DPU SKU differ from a BlueField-3 SuperNIC SKU?

A BlueField-3 DPU SKU, such as the BF3240, defaults to DPU mode (DPU_MODE_ECPF). The embedded Arm subsystem owns the NIC resources and the embedded switch. That ownership isolates host administration behind a DPU-controlled policy domain. A BlueField-3 SuperNIC SKU defaults to NIC mode (NIC_MODE_SUPERNIC). It exposes the Spectrum-X RoCEv2 data path directly to the host OS, with no intermediate Arm virtual-switch policy in the way.

8. Why can a Spectrum leaf port (swp14) report up at full speed while the attached host network interface (enp1s0f0np0) remains DOWN or DORMANT?

In DPU mode, swp14 up proves only Layer 3, the wire between the DPU and the leaf switch. If the Layer 2 Arm policy service (openvswitch-policy-load.service) fails during boot, the embedded switch never gets its forwarding rules. The Layer 1 host interface enp1s0f0np0 then stays DOWN.

11. In ADC Chapter 6, how does Static Load Balancing compute the outgoing ECMP link for 192.168.1.20 -> 192.168.2.10 (UDP 1024 -> 4791), and why does resilient hashing use 512 or 1,024 buckets?

The switch ASIC feeds the five-tuple hex string into a CRC32 generator, then takes the remainder modulo the number of active links. You can verify the arithmetic with the numbers below. Resilient hashing maps the hash into a fixed indirection table. When a link flaps, only the buckets that point to it are reassigned.

12. Given four flows of 50, 100, 50, and 100 Gbps arriving at a leaf with two 200 Gbps spine uplinks, compare link utilization under SLB versus DLB and Spectrum-X Adaptive Routing.

Under SLB, the two smaller flows hash onto SpineA at fifty percent utilization. The two larger flows collide on SpineB at full utilization. Under DLB or Spectrum-X Adaptive Routing, real-time load and queue depth balance both spines at seventy-five percent. That balance lifts cluster-wide effective bandwidth from roughly 60 percent to 95 percent.

SLB vs DLB / Spectrum-X Spine Balance (2 x 200 Gbps Spines, Flows = 50, 100, 50, 100 Gbps):
  - SLB:              SpineA = 50 + 50 = 100 Gbps (50% util); SpineB = 100 + 100 = 200 Gbps (100% util) -> ~60% cluster effective BW
  - DLB / Spectrum-X: SpineA = 150 Gbps (75% util); SpineB = 150 Gbps (75% util) -> ~95% cluster effective BW
13. How does Selective Dynamic Load Balancing decide which packets to spray per-packet versus per-flow?

Selective Dynamic Load Balancing uses an ingress TCAM ACL on the switch ASIC to match the RoCEv2 Base Transport Header opcode. Elephant flows with RDMA Write opcodes, between DDP-capable ConnectX or BlueField-3 endpoints, get PER_PACKET_SPRAY. Non-matching control or legacy flows stay on FLOWLET_OR_PER_FLOW, which preserves in-order arrival.

14. How does BlueField-3 or ConnectX-7/8 hardware out-of-order Direct Data Placement handle the arriving PSN stream (200, 202, 203, 201, 205, 204)?

Instead of dropping out-of-order packets and firing three PSN-gap NAKs, the SuperNIC computes each packet's destination GPU address from the base VA and the PSN offset. It places every payload directly in GPU memory with zero retransmissions. A hardware PSN bitmap tracks completion, and the CQE is posted only after all six packets are present.

Hardware OOO DDP Address Offsets (base_va = 0x7F0000000000, MTU = 4,096 B):
  - PSN 200 -> +0 B (0x7F0000000000)      | PSN 202 -> +8,192 B (0x7F0000002000, 0 NAKs vs NAK #1 on legacy NIC)
  - PSN 203 -> +12,288 B (0x7F0000003000) | PSN 201 -> +4,096 B (0x7F0000001000)
  - PSN 205 -> +20,480 B (0x7F0000005000) | PSN 204 -> +16,384 B (0x7F0000004000, bitmap 200..205 complete -> posts CQE)
15. How does Spectrum-X hardware telemetry-driven congestion control improve upon classical binary ECN/DCQCN?

Classical DCQCN uses a single-bit ECN mark. That forces a blind multiplicative rate cut, followed by slow probing back up. Spectrum-4 instead embeds exact hardware queue-depth and drain-rate telemetry in congestion feedback. BlueField-3 SuperNICs use it to set the exact fair-share drain rate within one round-trip time.

  - Classical Binary Cut:  Cuts 400.0 Gbps flow to 200.0 Gbps
  - Spectrum-X Telemetry:  Sets exact fair-share drain rate = 185.0 Gbps per flow in 1 RTT (CE = 11 binary probing replaced by exact queue telemetry)

18. Explain how a single switch port (swp19) configured with Priority trust state: pcp instead of dscp breaks a RoCEv2 (tos 0xb8) path without breaking ping.

The source host marks RoCEv2 packets with tos 0xb8, which is DSCP 46. Ingress leaf swp4, with trust dscp, maps that to lossless Priority 3.   - swp19 (Egress):  Priority trust state: pcp  -> untagged routed packet falls into lossy Priority 0
