Why this matters for AI infrastructure​

A platform team is asked to run the language models for the whole company. The support team wants a chat assistant, finance wants document summaries overnight, and an analytics group wants a large model for reports. Each team asked for its own GPUs, and nobody checked whether the sum was affordable. Together those requests cost more than the company can pay for, and the platform team is the one that has to say so.

That request is the system design question in this chapter. It is not "design a public API," which chapter 20.13 already covers for a vendor with outside customers. Here the customers are internal teams, the traffic is small, the models differ in size, and the pool is shared. The skill being tested is how you turn several unrelated demands into one pool that stays fast, stays cheap and stays safe to change. A design that serves one team well and the others badly has not solved the problem.

Picture the meeting where those requests land on one table. Each team brings a number that looks reasonable on its own. Put them side by side and the total is larger than the budget. The platform team's job in that meeting is not to refuse anyone. It is to show how the same pool can serve all of them, and to say what each team gives up when the pool is full.

As a capstone for the design track, this chapter is the vendor-neutral, platform-engineer's version of that design, written for interview pace. It builds on the chapters in Part 20 by link and does not repeat them. It adds one worked example and four small tested programs that you can run on a laptop. Decision rule: if you can say your sizing, your placement rule, your scaling signal and your rollback trigger as numbers, you have a design. If you can only say product names, you have a diagram.

In one minute: the design is a shared pool with priorities. Models are sized one by one with a tested estimator, then placed together so that their peaks do not coincide. Scaling and rollout are written as code with tests, so the rules are checked before they run.

Prerequisites and outcomes​

Prerequisites​

A colleague asks you to walk through the design, and you realise you cannot yet explain the KEDA trigger. Before you start, check that the building blocks below are familiar. Each one below is a chapter or a concept that this design relies on, and the design is much harder to follow without it.

  • Chapter 20.12, the estimation toolkit. This chapter imports its estimator.
  • Chapters 20.13 and 20.14, the multi-tenant API and rollout designs.
  • Chapters 20.19 and 20.20, SLOs and autoscaling.
  • Basic Kubernetes: Deployments, HPA, priority classes, namespaces.

Outcomes​

Picture the clock starting and the interviewer asking for your first number. The chapter follows the order you would speak the design in a real room. It starts with requirements and assumptions for a shared internal inference pool, stated in five minutes. Next it sizes each model with the 20.12 estimator and compares a shared pool with dedicated pools. It then chooses an engine and defends the choice by workload rather than by a benchmark. After that it places hot, warm, batch and cold models on one pool with priorities and an eviction rule. It writes the autoscaling policy and the canary plan as code that fails when a rule is broken. The last step is a design review with a checklist, followed by the 45-minute answer given out loud.

1. Requirements and the worked example (minutes 0 to 5)​

On Monday the finance team complains that the summaries are late. On Tuesday the support team complains that the assistant is slow at ten in the morning. Both teams use the same GPUs. Nobody wrote down what "fast" meant, so nobody can say who is right. Until someone writes the targets as numbers, every complaint is a feeling, and the platform team ends up arguing with feelings.

So the first job is to write the requirements as numbers. Ask five questions. Which models and which teams use them? What are the peak requests per second, and what are the prompt and answer lengths? What latency and availability does each team need? Is the data allowed to leave the company? Who pays, and how is use measured? The answers turn the vague complaint into a table you can compute on.

Then state your assumptions out loud. The table below is the worked example for the whole chapter. Every figure in it is my assumption for teaching, and none of it is a measurement of a real company. Read the table once as a set of inputs, and then ask which input would move the design most if it changed.

This framing differs from the public API in 20.13 in three ways. First, the tenants are teams you can talk to, so you can use quotas and chargeback, not only rate limits. Second, there are few models, so placement matters more than routing. Third, nobody sells the capacity, so cost is a budget you defend to finance. Each difference changes which tool you reach for first.

In one minute: an internal platform differs from a public API because tenants are known and models are few. Write four tiers of workload, state every number as an assumption, and say so out loud.

2. Sizing with the 20.12 estimator (minutes 5 to 11)​

The finance team asks for eight GPUs for the summaries. You answer with arithmetic, not with a feeling. Chapter 20.12 explains each formula, and this chapter does not redo them. The file c12_size_inference_pool.py imports capacity_estimator from code/part20 and calls its functions for each workload. A finance lead who hears a function name and a result can check the work later, which is the point.

The estimator gives a ceiling for one replica, in requests per second. It assumes that decode is limited by memory bandwidth and that prefill is limited by compute. It does not model tensor-parallel communication, so the 72-billion-parameter figure is the most optimistic of the three. Treat every result as an upper bound that you then check against a load test. A ceiling tells you what the hardware could do in theory, and a load test tells you what it does on your traffic.

WorkloadWeightsCeiling per replicaReplicas at peakGPUs at peak
assistant16.4 GB9.19 req/s44
docsum16.4 GB2.37 req/s44
analyst145.4 GB1.36 req/s416

The weights column explains a lot about the sizing. A 16.4 GB model fits on one 80 GB GPU, which leaves room for the cache. A 145.4 GB model does not fit one GPU at all, so it must be split across several. Because of that split, one replica of the analyst model consumes four GPUs before it serves a single request.

Replicas at peak use the 20.12 default of 90 percent target utilisation and no spare. Utilisation here means the share of a replica's capacity that you plan to use, and the rest is headroom for bursts. The assistant keeps a floor of two replicas, so one failure is not an outage. The 72B model does not fit on one GPU, so a replica takes four, and the script raises an error if you ask for fewer. A test covers that failure, so the rule cannot be broken by accident.

A spare exists because the pool must keep working on the worst day, not the average one. A node can fail, a driver can hang, or a rollout can take a node out of service. If the spare is smaller than the largest replica, the largest model is the first thing to go. Keeping a whole node in reserve is the simplest way to avoid that outcome.

Now the question that makes this a shared-pool design. Do the peaks coincide? The script holds an hourly profile for each model and adds the GPUs each model needs in each hour. If the peaks fall in different hours, one pool can serve both. If they fall together, the pool must be as big as their sum.

HoursGPUs neededWhat drives it
00:00 to 04:5910night batch at full rate
05:00 to 07:597floors only
10:00 to 11:5921assistant and analyst peaks together
14:00 to 15:5921second office peak
18:00 to 23:597floors only

Read the hourly table from top to bottom, as the day actually runs. At night the batch job takes the pool. In the morning the floors remain and the office traffic rises. Around midday the assistant and the analyst peak together, and that is the hour that sets the size of the pool. The afternoon brings a second office peak, which the table also puts at 21 GPUs. The evening falls back to the floors. The pool is sized for the busiest row, but it is paid for in every row.

The shared pool needs 21 GPUs at its busiest hour, plus one cold slot for the small fine-tunes. That is 22. Three dedicated pools, each sized for its own peak, need 24 plus the same slot, so 25. The saving in GPUs is small, only three. The big saving is not in the peak. It is in the hours when the pool shrinks, which section 8 prices.

A pool also needs spare capacity, because a pool with no spare fails the first time a node does. I choose one whole 8-GPU node, since losing a node must not take the largest model down with it. That brings the static pool to 30 GPUs. The packer places replicas first-fit by size, never splits a replica across nodes, and finds that the peak layout fills four nodes.

Decision rule: size each model alone, add the hourly demand, then add one node of spare. Say that the result is a ceiling, and that a load test replaces it.

In one minute: the estimator says 22 GPUs at peak against 25 for dedicated pools. Floors and the 72B model dominate the count. The real gain comes from letting the pool shrink at night.

3. Engine choice (minutes 11 to 14)​

Someone on the analytics team says, "we already use Triton, so use Triton." Someone else wants the fastest engine on a leaderboard. Both start from a tool instead of from the workload, and both will be disappointed when the tool does not match the traffic. The method below starts from the workload instead.

Hands-On LLM Serving and Optimization, chapter 8, gives a method. Write the targets first. Look at the real prompts and note whether they are prefill-heavy or decode-heavy. Compare engines with the same model, precision and batch. Measure start-up time, observability and upgrade effort too, because those decide how much work the team does every month. The book's closing advice is "workload first, not hype."

Its recommendations are short. vLLM is the fastest path to production, with broad model coverage. SGLang suits agent workloads and strict structured output. TensorRT-LLM suits teams standardised on NVIDIA that want the most tokens per dollar. llama.cpp suits local and edge use. The book also says to re-evaluate every three to six months and to keep an abstraction layer, so you can swap engines without rewriting the gateway.

For this pool I would pick vLLM for all four tiers. The reason is operational, not about speed. One engine means one set of metrics, one probe design and one upgrade path for five models. Chapter 20.22 has the flags, the metrics and the version-skew rules. I would revisit the choice for the analyst model first, because it uses the most GPUs and would gain most from a faster engine.

A server like Triton is a different layer from an engine. It can host many model types behind one API, including models that are not language models. This pool serves only language models, so I would not add it unless a non-LLM model joins the pool. Adding a second layer before it is needed only adds a place for bugs to hide.

Decision rule: pick one engine for the whole pool unless a measured gap on your own prompts justifies a second.

In one minute: choose by SLOs and operability, then fix one engine to keep operations simple. Put an abstraction at the gateway so the choice can change.

4. Batching and placement for several models (minutes 14 to 21)​

The overnight summary job starts at midnight and takes every free GPU. At seven the assistant is slow because the batch pods are still holding the nodes. The team asks why a batch job can hurt an interactive service. It is a fair question, and the answer shows how scheduling works when the cluster is full.

The answer is that nothing told the scheduler which workload matters more. Production Kubernetes, chapter 13, shows the pattern for cheap placeholder pods. Give them a lower priority, so any other pod pushes them out the moment it needs room. I use the same idea for the batch tier, with two priority classes, shown below.

# code/part19/c12_priority_classes.yaml
# Two priority classes for the shared pool. Interactive pods may preempt batch pods when GPUs run out.
# Values are examples. Batch pods must set priorityClassName: llm-batch, interactive pods llm-interactive.
apiVersion: scheduling.k8s.io/v1
kind: PriorityClass
metadata:
  name: llm-interactive
value: 1000
preemptionPolicy: PreemptLowerPriority
globalDefault: false
description: "Interactive LLM engines. They may preempt llm-batch pods."
---
apiVersion: scheduling.k8s.io/v1
kind: PriorityClass
metadata:
  name: llm-batch
value: 100
preemptionPolicy: Never
globalDefault: false
description: "Batch LLM engines. They wait for free GPUs and never preempt anyone."

Interactive pods may preempt, and batch pods never preempt anyone. Preempting means that a higher-priority pod evicts a lower-priority pod to free its GPUs. The Kubernetes documentation says a pod with preemptionPolicy: Never waits in the queue ahead of lower-priority pods and cannot evict others. It was fetched on 2026-11-10. The classes pass kubeconform. They were not applied to a cluster.

Four tiers follow from this. Each answers a different question about cold starts, which is the question of how long a request waits when the model is not already loaded. The table shows what each tier pays for that answer.

The names of the tiers are a shorthand, so keep them plain in the room. Hot means the model is always loaded. Warm means it is loaded but large and rarely called. Batch means it waits in a queue. Cold means it loads on demand. Once the team agrees on those four meanings, the rest of the discussion becomes about which tier each model belongs in, and that is a much easier conversation.

TierExampleMinimum replicasWhen it loadsWhat it gives up
hotassistant2 or more, always loadedat deploypays for idle GPUs at night
warmanalyst1, always loadedat deploypays for four idle GPUs
batchdocsum0when work is queuedwaits for free GPUs, can be preempted
coldcontracts-8b and two more0on first requestthe first request waits for the load

Each tier in this table is a promise about waiting time. A hot model promises an answer without a load, so it pays for idle GPUs. A cold model promises nothing about the first request, so it costs almost nothing while it sleeps. Choosing a tier is choosing which of those promises the team needs.

Hands-On LLM Serving and Optimization, chapter 3, describes the trade-off between two multi-model designs. A shared design saves money, but it suffers cold starts and trouble with scaling a hot model. A dedicated design for each model has no cold start and is simpler, but it wastes capacity on models that are used little. My tiers use both. Hot and warm models are dedicated, and cold models share slots.

The cold slots use a least-recently-used rule, the rule a model manager normally uses. When all slots are taken, the model unused for the longest time is unloaded to make room. The class ModelCache in the code implements it. The load time assumed here is 16.4 GB at 2 GB per second plus 45 seconds of engine start, about 53 seconds. These are assumptions. One source says a cold start can take several seconds or tens of seconds. Distributed AI Systems puts loading and warm-up at 30 to 60 seconds for LLMs, and Generative AI on Kubernetes says large models can take many minutes.

The trace in the code has 16 requests across the three small models. A trace is a recorded sequence of requests, and replaying it is a cheap way to test a cache without a GPU. The results show what a slot buys you.

Cold slotsHit rateTotal waitingEvictions
138 percent532 s9
250 percent426 s6
381 percent160 s0

A hit rate is the share of requests where the model is already loaded when the request arrives. A miss means a load, and a load means the request waits. The table shows the trade-off in one view. Each added slot raises the hit rate and cuts evictions, but each slot also holds a GPU that could serve traffic instead.

One slot thrashes when two teams alternate. Each request evicts the model the other team just loaded, so almost every request waits for a load. Three slots remove all evictions in this trace. Each slot is a GPU, so this is a price in GPUs set against a wait for the first request. I would start with two slots and watch the miss rate, then add a slot only if the waits hurt users.

Two more points belong here. First, a tensor-parallel replica must sit on one node, so a four-GPU replica never spans a node boundary. This is my reasoning from the link speed inside a node, and the packer enforces it. Second, when the small models are LoRA adapters on a shared base, they can live in one engine process. Generative AI on Kubernetes notes that this saves hardware but breaks the usual one-model-per-endpoint mapping in routing. My example uses full fine-tunes, so that case does not apply here.

Batching inside each engine is the engine's job, with continuous batching, as chapter 20.22 explains. The platform's job is to choose the design batch for each model, because the estimator and the autoscaler both use that number. Larger batches raise throughput and raise latency. LLMs in Production, chapter 6, says the same about adaptive batching, so the trade-off is not new.

Decision rule: give every model a tier, give each tier a priority class, and give the cold tier a slot count chosen from a measured miss rate.

In one minute: hot and warm models are dedicated, batch yields through a priority class, and cold models share LRU slots. One slot thrashes, three do not, and each slot costs one GPU.

5. Routing and the gateway (minutes 21 to 26)​

A new engineer on the support team points a script at the 72B model by mistake and runs a loop. Each call takes a whole replica's worth of GPUs for a long answer, and the queue grows behind it. The analyst team notices at once, because their replies slow down. The gateway should have stopped the loop before it reached the pool.

Distributed AI Systems, chapter 9, lists the parts. The gateway handles routing, authentication, rate limiting and load balancing. It is the single entry point, so every rule that applies to all traffic belongs there. Hands-On LLM Serving and Optimization, chapter 4, shows a gateway that checks a JWT or an API key, finds the tenant, applies a rate limit, and then picks an endpoint from a route table. The table can hold a weighted canary and tenant overrides.

I would keep five decisions at the gateway. Each one stops a problem that is cheaper to block at the front door than to clean up inside the pool.

  1. Authenticate every call, even internal ones. LLMs in Production, chapter 6, says internal access keys give a way to cut off a rogue process and to control cost.
  2. Map a model alias such as assistant to a version. Teams never see version numbers, so you can roll forward without asking them to change anything.
  3. Limit by tokens per team, not only by requests. A request count ignores how long each answer is. Chapter 20.13 has the token-bucket design and the metering.
  4. Attach a retry budget and a deadline. Chapter 20.21 explains why retries without a budget turn a partial outage into a full one.
  5. Reject early when the queue is full. Distributed AI Systems says to return 503 at once from a bounded queue, so clients see a clear signal instead of a long wait.

Inside one model, the router picks a replica. Distributed AI Systems names three balancers, which are round-robin, least connections and weighted. For LLMs, least in-flight requests is the sensible default, because request cost varies so much. One request may be a short question and the next a long report, so counting connections misjudges the load. When many requests share a long prefix, route by prefix to the replica that already holds that prefix in its cache. Generative AI on Kubernetes calls this prefix-aware routing, and chapter 20.21 compares it with load-aware routing. With only four replicas of a model, I would start with least in-flight and add prefix routing only if the cache hit rate is measured and worth the extra complexity.

Kubernetes offers the pieces. Distributed AI Systems lists Services and Ingress for load balancing, weighted routing for canaries, and readiness probes for health. KServe, KubeAI and llm-d are Kubernetes-native stacks that Distributed AI Systems compares. I would not choose one in an interview. I would say which problem each one solves and ask what the team already runs.

Decision rule: put identity, token quotas, aliases and deadlines at the gateway, and keep replica choice in the router.

In one minute: one gateway with five jobs. Inside a model, route to the least busy replica, and add prefix routing only when measurements justify it.

6. Autoscaling policy as code (minutes 26 to 31)​

At nine in the morning the assistant's queue grows. The autoscaler adds two replicas. They take two and a half minutes to become ready, and by then the queue has doubled. Everyone agrees the policy is wrong, but no one can say which number to change. A policy that cannot be read as a set of numbers cannot be tuned, so the team needs the rule written down.

Writing the policy as code fixes that. The function scaled_object() in c12_design_autoscaling.py builds a KEDA ScaledObject from the sizing, and check_policy() rejects policies that break the rules. KEDA is a Kubernetes add-on that scales a workload on an external metric. Chapter 20.20 explains the signals and the stabilization windows in depth. This chapter applies them to the shared pool.

Generative AI on Kubernetes, chapter 4, gives the background. The default HPA watches CPU and memory, which says little about an LLM engine that is waiting on the GPU. Knative's autoscaler counts requests, but one request may produce ten tokens and the next ten thousand. KEDA can scale on any query, and vLLM publishes the waiting-requests metric and the token timings. The same chapter notes that scale-to-zero is impractical for most LLM deployments, because loading takes minutes.

The policy derives its numbers from the sizing. Little's law says that the in-flight requests equal the rate times the time in the system. At the ceiling, the in-flight count equals the design batch. The target is 70 percent of that batch, which leaves room for growth during a cold start. For the assistant that is 45 requests per replica, from a batch of 64.

# code/part19/c12_inference_scaling.yaml
# KEDA ScaledObject for the hot model, generated by c12_design_autoscaling.scaled_object().
# Targets are derived from the sizing in c12_size_inference_pool.py: 70 percent of a batch of 64 is 45 in flight.
# Label selectors, the Prometheus address and the namespace are examples.
apiVersion: keda.sh/v1alpha1
kind: ScaledObject
metadata:
  name: vllm-assistant
  namespace: llm
spec:
  scaleTargetRef:
    name: vllm-assistant
  pollingInterval: 15
  minReplicaCount: 2
  maxReplicaCount: 8
  advanced:
    horizontalPodAutoscalerConfig:
      behavior:
        scaleUp:
          stabilizationWindowSeconds: 0
          policies:
          - type: Percent
            value: 100
            periodSeconds: 60
        scaleDown:
          stabilizationWindowSeconds: 900
          selectPolicy: Min
          policies:
          - type: Pods
            value: 1
            periodSeconds: 180
  triggers:
  - type: prometheus
    name: inflight
    metricType: AverageValue
    metadata:
      serverAddress: http://prometheus.monitoring.svc:9090
      query: sum(vllm:num_requests_running{namespace="llm",pod=~"vllm-assistant-.*"}) + sum(vllm:num_requests_waiting{namespace="llm",pod=~"vllm-assistant-.*"})
      threshold: '45'
      ignoreNullValues: 'false'
  - type: prometheus
    name: kv_cache
    metricType: Value
    metadata:
      serverAddress: http://prometheus.monitoring.svc:9090
      query: avg(vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc{namespace="llm",pod=~"vllm-assistant-.*"})
      threshold: '0.8'
      ignoreNullValues: 'false'
  fallback:
    failureThreshold: 3
    replicas: 2
    behavior: currentReplicas

The block is the file c12_inference_scaling.yaml. It was validated against the upstream KEDA CRD, using the repository kedacore/keda at commit 2132ca7 from 2026-11-07, cloned on 2026-11-10. The checker tools/check_crd_yaml.py reported 2 resources and 0 problems for this file and the batch file. It was not applied to a cluster. Label selectors and the address are examples, so replace them with your own before use.

Read the design choices one at a time, because each one answers a specific failure.

Taken together, these choices form one policy. The floor keeps the hot model alive, the windows keep it from flapping, the triggers catch both queueing and long prompts, and the fallback keeps the policy safe when its own input breaks. Changing one choice without checking the others is how a well-meant tweak creates a new outage.

  • The minimum of 2 comes from the hot tier rule. Why does a hot model need two replicas? One replica means one failure is an outage, so the floor is there to avoid that.
  • Scale-up has no delay. Scale-down waits 15 minutes and removes one replica every 3 minutes. My reasoning is that the window should be at least three cold starts, and the code computes a cold start of 150 seconds with a free node. A shorter window lets a short dip remove capacity that must then be rebuilt.
  • Two triggers ask for replicas, and the larger answer wins. In-flight requests catch queueing. KV-cache use catches long prompts, which fill memory before they fill the queue.
  • The fallback keeps two replicas if the metrics query fails three times. Without it, a broken query could freeze the fleet at the wrong size, and nobody would notice until users did.

The cold start of 150 seconds is the sum of an assumed 120 seconds to load weights and 30 seconds to warm up. Without a free node, add 240 seconds to get a node and 90 to pull the image, which gives 480 seconds. These are assumptions from the 20.20 simulator. Measure your own before you trust them.

That 330-second gap is why the pool keeps a spare node. Production Kubernetes, chapter 13, explains that the cluster autoscaler reacts to pods that cannot be scheduled, so the cluster is already full when it acts. Over-provisioning with low-priority placeholder pods keeps a node ready. The batch tier can play that role, because it is preempted first. For a ramp of 3 requests per second per minute, the code says one replica of headroom is enough with a free node, and three without one.

The batch workload is different. The file c12_inference_scaling_batch.yaml lets docsum scale to zero and wake when a queue appears. vLLM metrics do not exist when there are no pods, so the trigger reads a queue-depth metric that our own gateway would export. The metric name gateway_queue_depth is an example and was not run.

The difference matters for the trigger. A metric that exists only when pods exist cannot wake a pod that does not exist. So the batch trigger reads the queue in front of the pool, which the gateway can see even when the pool is at zero. That is a small design choice, and it is the reason the batch tier can sleep without losing work.

What the checker rejects is as important as what it generates. A policy file is easy to edit under pressure, and a wrong edit can look reasonable at first. Each of the rules below is a tested failure case, so a bad edit fails a test before it reaches the cluster.

Broken policyWhy it fails
hot model with minimum 0one scale-down empties the model
trigger on CPU or GPU utilisationutilisation does not track queueing
maximum replicas above the GPUs the model may usethe autoscaler will ask for GPUs that do not exist
scale-down window under three cold startsthe pool will flap
no fallbacka broken metric freezes scaling

Notice that every row in this table is a plausible mistake. A team might set the minimum to zero to save money, or copy a CPU-based autoscaler from another service. The table is useful because each row names the symptom in advance. When a real policy misbehaves, you can check it against these rows first.

Decision rule: derive every threshold from the sizing, write the policy as code, and let a test reject the five broken forms.

In one minute: scale on in-flight requests and KV-cache use, never on CPU or GPU utilisation. Keep a floor for hot models, a slow scale-down, a fallback, and a spare node.

7. Canary and rollback as code (minutes 31 to 36)​

The data science team ships a new version of the assistant model. Latency is the same and errors are the same. A week later users complain that answers are cut short. Nobody looked at how the answers ended, so the problem went unseen for days.

Chapter 20.14 already has the gate logic. Its decide() function compares a canary with the baseline on error rate, time to first token, inter-token latency, the share of answers that stop on length, and a quality score. The file c12_design_canary.py imports decide, Gates and Metrics from it, and it does not copy them. Distributed AI Systems, chapter 9, describes the same ladder, with a canary at 10 percent and a gradual shift to 100.

What the shared pool adds is three things. Each one is a constraint that a dedicated pool does not face in the same way.

First, the canary needs GPUs. A plan for the assistant at 30 QPS gives the ladder below. Each step needs the replicas to carry its share at 90 percent utilisation. On a dedicated pool a canary can simply take extra capacity, but on a shared pool it competes with every other model for the same spare.

Traffic to canaryCanary replicasExtra GPUs
5 percent11
25 percent11
50 percent22
100 percent44

The ladder makes the cost of a canary visible. At 5 percent of traffic the canary needs one GPU, and at full traffic it needs four GPUs, which matches the assistant's four replicas at peak. That growth is why a canary on a shared pool needs a plan for its GPUs before the first step, not after.

With 8 spare GPUs every step fits. With only 1 spare, the plan stops at 50 percent and returns "hold." The ladder is a teaching choice. The book's ladder starts at 10 percent, and this chapter's first step is 5 percent, which costs one GPU on this pool.

Second, a canary can hurt its neighbours. If the canary takes GPUs by preempting batch pods, or loads a different engine build on a shared node, the analyst may slow down. The function neighbour_impact() compares the time to first token of every other model before and during the canary. A rise above 20 percent forces a rollback, even when the canary looks healthy. A test shows this case.

Neighbour effects are easy to miss because each model looks healthy on its own dashboard. A canary that takes GPUs from a batch pod may be perfectly fine, while the analyst's first-token time quietly rises. The neighbour check looks at the other models at the same time, so the slowdown is caught in the window where a rollback still helps.

Third, rollback has an order. The function rollback_order() returns three steps. Set the canary weight to zero at the gateway. Wait for in-flight requests to finish, then scale the canary to zero. Return the GPUs and resume any paused batch pods. Traffic comes first because it stops the harm at once.

The tests cover the cases that matter. A good canary promotes. A quality drop rolls back. Too few requests holds. A failing step at 25 percent stops the ladder there. A pool with too few spare GPUs holds before the step that does not fit. Each test names one rule, so a failure points at a single decision.

Distributed AI Systems also separates canary from A/B tests. A canary is about safety, and it asks whether the new version is harmful. An A/B test compares options and needs the same user to see the same variant every time. For model upgrades inside a company, use the canary first, and reserve the A/B test for questions about which option users prefer.

Decision rule: a version moves up the ladder only if its error, latency, finish-reason and quality gates pass and no neighbour got slower.

In one minute: import the gate logic, add a plan that fits the spare GPUs, a neighbour check and a fixed rollback order. A test proves each one, including the failures.

8. Cost and utilisation (minutes 36 to 39)​

Finance reads the bill and asks why the GPUs cost so much when the dashboards show them mostly idle. The truthful answer is that they pay for the peak of the busiest hour, all day. A GPU that is idle at night still costs money at night. That is the number this section works through.

The script prices three designs with an assumed $4.50 per GPU-hour. That is the Nebius on-demand H100 list price that chapter 20.12 cites from nebius.com/prices, fetched 2026-11-09, so treat it as a placeholder. Use your own contract rate. A month has 30 days.

DesignGPUs paidUtilisationCost per month
three dedicated pools plus spare node33, all dayn/a$106,920
one shared pool, fixed at peak plus spare30, all day20 percent$97,200
one shared pool that follows the load30 at most, fewer off-peak29 percent$66,285

The three rows differ in one thing that matters most, which is whether the GPU count follows the load. The first two keep the same count all day, and the third lets the count fall at night. The utilisation column shows the cost of that choice in one number, and the cost per token that follows is the part finance will ask about.

The busy fraction is 143 GPU-hours of real work per day, from the estimator's ceilings. Utilisation here means that work divided by the GPU-hours paid. It is not the number nvidia-smi prints, because that tool reports the instantaneous state of the device, not the share of paid time that did useful work.

The big step is the last row. A fixed pool saves $9,720 over three dedicated pools, because the peaks do not coincide. A pool that follows the load saves $40,635, because the night hours are cheap. That saving depends on scaling down safely, which section 6 covers, and on the spare node being a placeholder that other work can use while it sits idle.

Utilisation also sets the price of a token. The formula from 20.12 gives the assistant $0.45 per million output tokens at full utilisation. At the pool's 29 percent it is $1.56. At 20 percent it is $2.28. The GPU price did not change. The idle time did, and the idle time is what the team controls.

Distributed AI Systems says spot or preemptible capacity can cost 60 to 90 percent less and suggests running about half of baseline capacity on it. I would only put the batch tier on spot, because it can wait. Do not put hot models there. Chapter 6 of LLMs in Production adds a warning from experience. GPUs are scarce, and a scale-up can fail because a whole region has none left. A spare node is partly insurance against that.

Finance also wants chargeback, which means charging each team for what it used. The gateway already counts tokens per team and model, so a monthly report is a sum over its logs. Charge input and output tokens separately, because prefill and decode cost different GPU time, and a team that writes long prompts should see that in its bill.

Chargeback is also a way to make cost visible to the teams that create it. Seeing its own token bill gives a team a reason to look at its usage. The gateway data that makes the report possible is the same data the team needs to tune its own usage, so the report is worth building early.

Decision rule: quote cost with its utilisation, and say which lever you pull first: let the pool shrink, then batch on spot, then quantise.

In one minute: sharing saves 9 percent at peak and following the load saves 38 percent. The cost per token is set by idle time, not by the GPU price.

9. Security (minutes 39 to 41)​

A contractor's laptop token ends up in a public repository. It can call the 72B model. The cost is small this time. The next token might read another team's prompts. This is why the security section starts from the assumption that a credential will leak, and asks what the leak can reach.

Production Kubernetes, chapter 12, treats tenancy as a spectrum. It lists namespaces as the base boundary, with resource quotas, RBAC and network policies built on them. It recommends a default deny-all network policy so that tenants must declare the traffic they need. It also warns that container isolation is weaker than a hypervisor, and that noisy neighbours are hard to remove fully.

For this pool I would do five things. Each one closes a path that a leaked credential or a bad image could use.

  1. Run engines in their own namespace with a default deny-all network policy. Only the gateway may reach them.
  2. Give each team a gateway credential with a model allow-list and a token quota. Revoke one team without touching the rest.
  3. Keep weights out of the image. LLMs in Production, chapter 6, calls baking a model into an image an antipattern. It also names the security cost, because the image is easy to reach. Mount weights from a volume or a local cache instead.
  4. Pin the engine image by version or digest. Generative AI on Kubernetes says never to use a mutable tag such as latest.
  5. Apply input and output guards in the application layer, not in the pool. Machine Learning Platform Engineering, chapter 13, describes guards for personal data and prompt injection around the model.

One caution. Production Kubernetes presents Pod Security Policies. That API was removed from Kubernetes, and the book predates its replacement. Use Pod Security Admission instead. Its documentation was not checked here, so treat that line as unverified until someone reads it.

Secrets in the examples are placeholders only. A real gateway key lives in a secret store, readable only by the gateway service account. If it is mounted as a file, the file mode should be 0400, so that only the owner can read it.

Decision rule: one namespace for engines, default deny, credentials per team, and no weights or secrets in images.

In one minute: isolate the engines, authenticate every team, keep weights and keys out of images, and put content guards in the application.

10. Observability and failure handling (minutes 41 to 45)​

At 14:10 the analyst's replies stop. The dashboard shows the pool at 60 percent GPU use and no errors. A node died, the 72B replica on it vanished, and the second replica was already full. The pool looked healthy on average, and the failure only showed up in one model's replies.

Start with what to watch. Distributed AI Systems names three kinds of telemetry: traces, metrics and structured logs. Its key LLM metrics are requests per second by model, latency percentiles, active requests and GPU use. Generative AI on Kubernetes adds time to first token, inter-token latency, queue depth and KV-cache use. The SLO side is in 20.19. Measure at the gateway and explain at the engine, so that a symptom and its cause are never confused.

A useful rule for dashboards is to watch the gateway for symptoms and the engine for causes. Users feel the gateway, so its latency and error rates tell you that something is wrong. The engine tells you why, through queue depth, cache use and GPU activity. Watching both lets you move from "users are waiting" to "the cache is full" in a few clicks.

The failure modes are in 20.18. For this design I would rehearse four. Rehearsing means walking through the failure on paper, naming the signal you would see and the action you would take, before it happens for real.

FailureWhat users seeThe design's answer
node lossthe 72B model drops to one replicaspare node and priority preemption of batch
cold-model missthe first call of a rare model waits about a minuteLRU slots, a timeout and a clear error
metrics outagescaling freezesthe fallback replica count in the policy
bad canaryanswers get shorter or slowergate, rollback order, neighbour check

The table turns failures into rehearsals. For each row, picture the user first, then the signal on the dashboard, then the action the design takes. A failure you have rehearsed on paper is much less frightening when it happens at night.

Node loss deserves the arithmetic. The 72B replica needs four GPUs. A spare node holds eight. So the spare absorbs one node loss, and batch pods on the other nodes can be preempted for a second. The review checklist flags a pool whose spare is smaller than its largest replica, because such a pool cannot recover from its worst case.

Streams also need care on scale-down. If the termination grace period is shorter than the longest request, replies are cut mid-stream. The user sees half an answer and no error. The checklist tests that as well, because it is the kind of failure that a dashboard rarely shows.

The file c12_design_review.py is the checklist as code. It takes a design as a plain dictionary and returns findings, each tied to a section of this chapter. The good example design has no findings. The bad example has 21, across eight sections. Use it in a real review as a list of questions, not as a pass mark. The checks are my reasoning and not an external standard.

Decision rule: for every component on the diagram, name the failure, the signal that shows it and the action that fixes it.

In one minute: watch the gateway for symptoms and the engine for causes. Rehearse node loss, cold misses, metric loss and a bad canary, and run the checklist before you ship.

How to answer out loud​

You sit down, and the interviewer says: "Design inference serving for a company with several internal models and a shared GPU pool. You have 45 minutes." The question is broad on purpose. A strong answer starts from the numbers, not from the boxes, and it stays inside the time.

Start with the requirements and the numbers. Say that every number is an assumption and that you will change it if the interviewer gives you different ones. Then walk the sections in order, and stop at the end of each block for the interviewer to redirect you if they wish.

Use this table as a pacing guide during the real round. Each row gives the minutes, the topic and the numbers that the interviewer is most likely to remember. If you run short on time, the numbers in the right-hand column are the ones to keep.

Say the trade-offs plainly. Shared pools cost less and risk cold starts. Dedicated pools are simpler and waste GPUs. A larger spare node costs money every hour and saves you on the worst day. A longer scale-down window wastes some GPU time and prevents flapping. The interviewer is listening for a candidate who can name the cost of each choice without being prompted.

Expect these follow-ups, and answer each in one or two sentences. A short answer that shows the method is stronger than a long answer that repeats the diagram.

  • "What if the 72B model's traffic triples?" Recompute with the estimator. Its replicas grow in fours, so the pool grows by half a node at a time.
  • "What if finance wants guaranteed latency?" Move the batch tier to spot, move the assistant to a dedicated pool, and show the cost of doing so.
  • "How do you know the estimate is right?" It is a ceiling. Load test one replica and replace the ceiling with the measurement.
  • "What would you do first on Monday?" Write the SLOs and the metric list, because the rest of the design depends on them.

Do not say "it depends" and stop, because that answer closes the conversation. Say what it depends on, which number would change the answer, and what you would measure first. Do not say "we use product X" without a reason. Do not claim a number you did not state as an assumption.

Mistakes to avoid​

Picture a candidate who draws the architecture in the first minute and runs out of time before any number appears. The list below names that mistake and the others like it. Each one is easy to avoid once you have seen it happen.

  • Drawing the architecture before writing numbers, which makes the diagram look confident and empty.
  • Scaling on CPU or GPU utilisation, which does not track queueing.
  • Giving every model its own pool without computing the peak sum, which wastes GPUs at night.
  • Letting batch work share GPUs with no priority rule, which hurts the interactive tier.
  • Promoting a canary on latency and errors alone, which misses shortened answers.
  • Forgetting that the autoscaler reacts after the cluster is full, which makes scaling look slower than it is.
  • Quoting cost without utilisation, which hides the idle time that drives the bill.

Checkpoint questions​

1. Why is this design different from a public multi-tenant API?

The tenants are internal teams you can talk to, so quotas and chargeback work. There are few models, so placement matters more than routing. Cost is a budget, not revenue.

2. What are the first numbers you write down?

Peak QPS, prompt and answer lengths, latency and availability targets for each model, and the GPU type. You also say they are assumptions.

3. Where does the sizing code get its per-replica capacity?

It imports capacity_estimator from code/part20, the 20.12 estimator, and calls its functions. A test checks the result against a direct call.

4. Why is the estimator's answer a ceiling?

It assumes bandwidth-bound decode and compute-bound prefill at an assumed utilisation. It ignores tensor-parallel communication and real scheduling, so a load test must replace it.

5. Why does the 72B model need four GPUs per replica in this design?

Its BF16 weights are about 145 GB, which does not fit one 80 GB GPU. The script raises an error when asked to place it on one.

6. Why is the shared pool only three GPUs smaller at peak?

The hot floor and the 72B replicas dominate the count, and two peaks coincide around midday. The larger saving comes from the quiet hours.

7. How should you choose an engine?

Start from SLOs, test real prompts, compare engines under the same settings, and measure operability. The rule is workload first, not hype.

8. Why choose one engine for the whole pool?

One engine means one set of metrics, probes and upgrade steps. Revisit the choice when a measured gap on your prompts justifies it.

9. What does the batch priority class prevent?

It stops batch pods from evicting interactive pods. With preemptionPolicy: Never they wait for free GPUs. Interactive pods can preempt them.

10. What are the four tiers and what does each give up?

Hot pays for idle GPUs, warm pays for idle GPUs at a larger size, batch waits and may be preempted, and cold makes its first request wait for a load.

11. What does an LRU slot do for cold models?

When all slots are taken, it unloads the model used least recently and loads the requested one. More slots raise the hit rate and cost one GPU each.

12. What five jobs does the gateway do?

Authenticate, map aliases to versions, limit tokens per team, attach retry budgets and deadlines, and reject early when the queue is full.

13. Why scale on in-flight requests and KV-cache use?

CPU and GPU utilisation do not track queueing, and request counts ignore token length. In-flight requests and KV-cache use track the real load.

14. How is the in-flight target derived?

By Little's law, in-flight equals rate times time. At the ceiling that equals the design batch, so the target is 70 percent of it. For the assistant that is 45.

15. Why does the scale-down window have a floor?

A replica takes minutes to return. If the window is shorter than three cold starts, a short dip removes capacity that must be rebuilt, and the pool flaps.

16. What does the canary plan check beyond the 20.14 gates?

That the canary's GPUs fit the spare, and that no neighbouring model's time to first token rose more than 20 percent.

17. What is the rollback order?

Set the canary weight to zero, drain and scale it down, then return the GPUs and resume paused batch pods. Traffic comes first.

18. What decides the cost per token?

Utilisation. The same GPU gives $0.45 per million output tokens at full use and $1.56 at 29 percent, in this example.

19. What does a default deny network policy achieve?

Nothing may reach the engines until a rule allows it. Only the gateway gets a rule, and teams cannot call engines directly.

20. What does the spare node protect against?

Loss of a node that holds the largest replica, and a scale-up that cannot find GPUs. It must be at least the size of the largest replica.
